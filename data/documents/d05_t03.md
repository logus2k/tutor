# Create Effective System-Level Instructions

System-level instructions — what claude.ai calls **profile instructions** (account-wide) and **project instructions** (per workspace) — are the standing guidance Claude reads before it responds. Writing them well is one of the highest-leverage skills a business user has: a good instruction set makes every future conversation better without repeating yourself. This topic tests whether you can write clear, durable instructions and can tell what belongs in instructions versus a single prompt.

## What "system-level" instructions are

There are two layers, and knowing which to use is itself an exam-worthy judgment:

- **Profile instructions** apply to *all* your conversations. Set them in Settings for things that are true no matter what you're working on — your role, your organization, terms and abbreviations you use, and how you like Claude to communicate. Anthropic describes these as your "preferred approaches or methods, common terms or concepts you use, typical scenarios you encounter, or general communication instructions."
- **Project instructions** apply only inside one Project. Use them to "provide project-specific context, set guidelines for a particular workflow, establish requirements for a specific set of tasks, or define roles or perspectives Claude should adopt."

The mental model: profile instructions are *who you are and how you like to work*; project instructions are *the rules of this particular job*; the prompt is *this specific request right now*.

## What good instructions contain

Effective instructions are specific, durable, and testable. Cover these elements, roughly in this order:

1. **Role / persona.** Tell Claude what expert stance to take. "You are a grants officer reviewing applications against our funding criteria" is far more useful than a blank slate, because it orients tone, vocabulary, and priorities.
2. **Audience.** Who reads the output? "Explain for a non-technical hospital administrator" and "Write for a panel of actuaries" produce very different, correctly-calibrated results.
3. **Tone and voice.** "Professional but warm; short sentences; no marketing hype." If you have a house style, name it or attach it.
4. **Constraints and do-not rules.** These are often the most valuable line. "Never invent statistics or citations; if a figure isn't in the provided documents, say you don't know." "Do not give legal advice; flag anything that needs counsel." Explicit *don'ts* prevent the confident, plausible-but-wrong output that Claude can otherwise produce.
5. **Output format preferences.** "Default to a two-sentence summary, then a bulleted action list." "Always end with the assumptions you made." Making the default format explicit saves you from reformatting every reply.
6. **Process expectations.** "Ask clarifying questions before drafting if the request is ambiguous" is a powerful instruction that reduces wasted, off-target answers.

## Write instructions the way you'd brief a capable new hire

The most reliable heuristic: imagine onboarding a smart, fast colleague who knows your field in general but nothing about *your* context. You would tell them your role, your audience, your non-negotiables, and how you like work delivered — and you would be concrete, not vague. "Be professional" is weak; "Use British spelling, avoid exclamation marks, and never promise timelines" is strong because Claude can actually act on it and you can check whether it did.

Two techniques sharpen instructions:

- **Be positive and concrete about what you *do* want**, alongside the don'ts. "Lead with the recommendation, then the reasoning" beats "don't bury the point."
- **Give a short example when a rule is hard to state.** One sample of an ideal answer communicates tone and structure faster than a paragraph describing them.

## What belongs in instructions vs. a single prompt

This is the distinction the exam is most likely to test. Put something in **instructions** when it is *durable* — true across many future conversations. Put it in a **prompt** when it is *specific to this one task*.

- Belongs in instructions: your role, your audience, your standing tone and format, your do-not rules, recurring context ("we operate in the EU, so assume GDPR applies").
- Belongs in the prompt: "Summarize *this* report in 200 words," "make *this* paragraph more formal," "draft a reply to *this* email."

A common mistake is stuffing a one-off request into the Project instructions ("summarize the Q3 deck"). It doesn't belong there — it will confuse every unrelated future chat in that Project. The reverse mistake is re-pasting your role and style rules into every prompt; that's exactly what instructions exist to eliminate. If you find yourself typing the same preamble repeatedly, promote it to instructions.

## Keep instructions durable and lean

Instructions are read on *every* relevant conversation, so bloat has a cost: contradictory or excessive rules dilute the ones that matter. Favor a tight set of clear, high-value directives over an exhaustive list. When two instructions conflict, Claude has to guess — so keep them consistent and remove rules that no longer apply.

Instructions also shape reliability. If accuracy matters for your audience, encode that as a rule: "Only state facts supported by the provided documents; mark anything uncertain as an assumption." This does not make Claude infallible — it can still generate fluent, wrong-but-confident details, and its confident tone is not a trustworthiness signal — but a well-written constraint reduces the failure rate and makes verification easier. For compliance or high-stakes work, instructions complement, not replace, your own review.

## A worked example

Weak: "Help me with customer emails. Be nice."

Strong (project instruction): "You are a customer-support specialist for a B2B software company. Audience: paying business customers, sometimes frustrated. Tone: calm, empathetic, solution-first; British English. Always acknowledge the issue in the first sentence, then give concrete next steps. Never promise refunds, delivery dates, or features not in the provided help docs — if asked, say you'll escalate to a human. End each reply offering one further point of contact."

The strong version fixes role, audience, tone, format, and — critically — the do-not rules and the escalation path, so every draft in the Project starts calibrated.
