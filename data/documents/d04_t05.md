# Communicate Claude's Value and Limitations to Stakeholders

Getting value from Claude in an organization depends as much on how you talk about it as on how you use it. This topic tests whether you can make an honest case for Claude — real benefits *and* real limits — set expectations that survive contact with reality, and tell stakeholders exactly where human oversight and escalation are required.

## Articulating the value honestly

Stakeholders (a manager, a client, a review board) want to know what they'll get. Frame value in *business terms*, not features: time saved, faster turnaround, more consistent quality, wider option-generation, fewer routine handoffs. Concrete framing lands better than hype: "Claude drafts the first version of our weekly report in minutes, so the analyst spends their time verifying and interpreting instead of assembling" is more credible than "AI will transform our reporting."

Ground the value in what Claude genuinely does well: summarizing and synthesizing long documents, drafting and rewriting content, structuring messy information, comparing options, researching across many sources with citations, and prototyping tangible deliverables like dashboards and templates through Artifacts. Anthropic positions Claude as a productivity layer for knowledge work, and the honest pitch stays inside that lane.

The tempting mistake is to oversell — "it's basically an autonomous employee," "it's always right," "we can fire the review step." Overselling sets a bar Claude will miss, and the first visible error then destroys trust in the whole initiative. Underselling is the opposite failure: dismissing Claude as "just a chatbot" leaves real, safe gains on the table. Aim for the calibrated middle: specific benefits, named limits.

## Being honest about limitations

Credibility comes from naming the limits *before* a stakeholder discovers them. The ones that matter most for a business audience:

- **It can fabricate specific-looking details — "hallucination."** Claude can produce a citation, a statistic, a subsection number, or a quote that looks authoritative and is simply wrong. This is the single most important limitation to communicate, because the output's polish hides the error.
- **Confidence is not accuracy.** Claude may state something in a certain, fluent tone whether or not it's correct. Its self-reported confidence is **not** a reliability signal, so "it sounded sure" is never a reason to skip verification.
- **Knowledge and access have boundaries.** Claude doesn't automatically know your internal systems, this week's private events, or anything behind a system it isn't connected to; without supplied data or a connector it may fill gaps with plausible guesses.
- **Data handling has rules.** Regulated or personal data must be handled per policy *before* use — anonymized or cleared — not on trust. Telling Claude "don't retain this" is not a data-governance control and shouldn't be presented to stakeholders as one.
- **It's a tool, not a decision-maker.** Claude informs judgment; it doesn't own outcomes.

Naming these limits is not a weakness in your pitch — it's what lets a cautious stakeholder say yes, because they can see you've bounded the risk.

## Setting expectations that hold up

Translate value and limits into concrete expectations:

- **Show the human's role.** Make clear that a person reviews and verifies before anything high-stakes ships. Stakeholders trust "Claude drafts, a named human approves" far more than "the AI handles it."
- **Define what "good" looks like.** Tie expectations to checkable acceptance criteria ("summaries under 200 words, every figure traceable to the source") so success isn't subjective.
- **Frame it as a fast draft-and-verify loop**, not a magic answer button. This primes stakeholders to expect iteration and a verification step rather than instant perfection.
- **Right-size the tooling story.** If cost or speed is a stakeholder concern, explain that the model tier is matched to the task — a faster, cheaper tier for simple high-volume steps, a more capable tier reserved for genuinely complex work — so they hear cost-discipline, not "always use the biggest model."

## Where human oversight and escalation are needed

A large part of communicating limits is stating *precisely where a human must stay in the loop* and *when to escalate*. Give stakeholders a clear map:

- **Always human-reviewed before release:** anything legal, financial, medical, HR, compliance-facing, or otherwise high-stakes and hard to reverse. The reviewer verifies factual claims against an authoritative source.
- **Escalate to a human expert** when the task exceeds Claude's reliable range — a genuine legal, medical, or financial judgment — or when Claude's output is uncertain, internally contradictory, or the stakes are high enough that being wrong is unacceptable.
- **Escalate to a technical specialist** when the need crosses from business use into system integration, automated actions with real-world effect, or sensitive-data connections — decisions with security and governance weight that a business user shouldn't make alone.
- **Escalate on data sensitivity** whenever a use case would put regulated or personal data into Claude before it's been properly handled — pause and get the control right first.

The disciplined message to stakeholders is: "Claude accelerates the work; humans own the decisions and verify the facts; here are the exact points where we stop and escalate." A pitch that omits the escalation map is the one the exam marks wrong, because it leaves the audience without the guardrails that make adoption safe.

### A worked mini-example

Pitching Claude to a compliance-conscious operations lead: "It will draft our vendor-communication emails and summarize contract changes, cutting turnaround roughly in half. Two things to know: it can occasionally state a wrong detail confidently, so every summary is verified against the actual contract before we act, and we anonymize any personal data before it goes in. Anything with legal weight is reviewed by our counsel, not sent automatically." That message pairs a concrete benefit with the two most important limits and a clear oversight boundary — which is exactly the calibrated, honest framing this topic rewards. The wrong version — "it'll handle all our vendor comms automatically and it's very accurate" — wins the meeting and loses the trust the moment a fabricated detail slips through.

## Common pitfalls the exam probes

- Overselling ("autonomous," "always accurate") or underselling ("just a chatbot").
- Presenting Claude's confident tone as evidence of correctness.
- Omitting the human-review and escalation points from the pitch.
- Claiming a data-privacy control ("we told it not to remember") that isn't one.
- Talking features instead of business value the stakeholder actually cares about.
