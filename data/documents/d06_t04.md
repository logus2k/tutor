# Understand the Ethical Implications of AI Usage

Beyond what is allowed by policy lies the harder question of what is *right and responsible*. The Foundations exam treats ethics as a practical, everyday competency: recognizing where AI can cause harm through bias, opacity, or misplaced trust, and building the human oversight that keeps its use fair and accountable. These are judgment questions, and the exam scenarios reward the responsible choice over the merely permissible one.

## Bias and fairness

AI models learn from large bodies of human-generated text, and human text carries human bias. That means Claude can reproduce or amplify unfair patterns — in how it describes groups of people, ranks candidates, or frames a decision — even when no one intended it. The ethical risk is highest exactly where the stakes are highest: hiring, lending, housing, healthcare, and criminal justice, where a skewed output can systematically disadvantage real people.

The responsible practice is not to assume the model is neutral. Watch for outputs that make assumptions based on names, gender, ethnicity, age, or geography; test consequential prompts for disparate treatment; and keep a human decision-maker between the model's suggestion and any outcome that affects a person. A worked example: asking Claude to screen résumés and *rank* candidates risks encoding bias into a consequential decision. The responsible pattern is to use Claude to *summarize* qualifications against explicit, job-relevant criteria while a qualified person makes and owns the selection — which is also what Anthropic's high-risk-use rules require.

## Transparency and disclosure

People have a legitimate interest in knowing when they are dealing with AI rather than a human, and when a decision or communication that affects them was AI-assisted. Anthropic's Usage Policy builds this in: consumer-facing chatbots must disclose that a user is interacting with an AI, and high-risk uses require disclosing AI involvement at least at the start of each session. Ethically, the principle is broader than the rule — do not pass off AI-generated work as human when the audience's trust depends on knowing the difference, and do not let AI impersonate a real person. Honest disclosure preserves the trust that makes the tool useful in the first place.

## Over-reliance and appropriate trust

Perhaps the most common everyday ethical failure is **over-reliance**: treating fluent, confident output as if it were verified truth. Claude can fabricate specific-looking details — citations, statistics, subsection numbers, quotations — that read as authoritative but are wrong. Critically, a model's confident tone is *not* a reliability signal; it can be equally confident when right and when hallucinating.

Appropriate trust means calibrating your reliance to the stakes. For low-stakes drafting and brainstorming, a quick sanity check is enough. For anything factual, high-stakes, or bound for a compliance, legal, or executive audience, verify claims against an authoritative source before you act or send. The exam's expected judgment: never forward AI-generated facts or citations to a high-stakes audience without independent verification. The tempting-but-wrong alternative — "the model sounded sure, so it must be right" — is precisely the reasoning error the exam is testing.

Over-reliance also has a skills dimension: outsourcing thinking entirely can erode a professional's own judgment and let errors pass unchecked. The healthy stance is Claude as a capable assistant that accelerates your work, with you remaining the expert who evaluates it.

## Human oversight and accountability

The connective thread through all of these is that a human must stay meaningfully in control of consequential uses, and a human — never the model — is accountable for the result. Anthropic's framework operationalizes this with human-in-the-loop requirements for high-risk domains: a qualified professional must review AI-assisted content or decisions before they are finalized or disseminated. Ethically, "the AI did it" is never a valid excuse; the person who deploys the output owns its consequences.

Meaningful oversight is more than a rubber stamp. It means a qualified reviewer who understands the domain, actually examines the output, has the authority and the information to override it, and takes responsibility for what ships. A reviewer who approves without reading provides the appearance of oversight without the substance — a pattern the exam would mark as inadequate.

## Weighing benefit against harm

Responsible use also means asking who could be harmed and whether the benefit justifies the risk — including harms the immediate user does not see: the person whose data is exposed, the candidate filtered out unfairly, the reader misled by an undisclosed AI byline, the individual on the receiving end of an automated decision. When a use could materially harm someone, the ethical thresholds rise: stronger verification, explicit disclosure, human review, and sometimes the decision not to automate at all. Anthropic's own responsible-development posture — safety testing, usage restrictions, and transparency commitments — models this proportional, harm-aware mindset that the exam expects candidates to apply at their own scale.

## An ethical self-check for any AI use

1. **Fairness** — could this output be biased or treat people unequally? Have I kept a human between the suggestion and any decision about a person?
2. **Transparency** — does my audience know AI was involved where that matters, and am I not misrepresenting AI work as human?
3. **Trust** — have I verified the facts and citations to a degree that matches the stakes, rather than trusting a confident tone?
4. **Oversight** — is a qualified human genuinely reviewing consequential output, with authority to override it?
5. **Accountability** — is it clear that I, not the model, own the result and its consequences?

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- Assuming the model is neutral and letting it make or rank consequential decisions about people.
- Presenting AI-generated content as human, or skipping required AI disclosure.
- Trusting confident, fluent output without verifying facts and citations for high-stakes audiences.
- Providing token oversight — approving without genuinely reviewing.
- Treating the model as the responsible party instead of the human using it.
