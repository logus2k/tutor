# Adapt Prompting Strategies Based on Task Type

Good prompting is not one fixed recipe. The instructions that produce a sharp analysis are different from the ones that produce a lively brainstorm, and asking for the wrong thing wastes the interaction. The Foundations exam tests whether you can recognize the *type* of task in front of you — analysis, research, drafting, or brainstorming — and shape the prompt to match. Each task type has a different goal, a different failure mode, and therefore a different set of things worth asking for.

## Why task type changes the prompt

Every prompt encodes an implicit definition of success. For an analysis the success is *accuracy and defensible reasoning*; for a brainstorm it is *quantity and range*. If you prompt a brainstorm the way you prompt an analysis — demanding one correct, well-justified answer — you will strangle the creativity you wanted. If you prompt an analysis the way you prompt a brainstorm — "give me lots of ideas" — you will get breadth where you needed rigor. Naming the task type first tells you which levers (grounding, structure, tone, divergence) to pull.

## Analysis

Analytical tasks — interpreting a dataset, reviewing a contract, evaluating options against criteria — live or die on being grounded and structured. The overriding risk is a confident answer that is not actually supported by the material.

What to ask for:

- **Ground the work in the source.** Have Claude quote or cite the specific passages or figures it is reasoning from before it draws conclusions. The docs recommend exactly this for document tasks: pull the relevant quotes first, then analyze. It cuts through noise and makes the reasoning auditable.
- **Make the reasoning explicit and stepwise.** Ask Claude to work through the analysis in stages rather than jumping to a verdict, so you can see and check the logic.
- **State the evaluation criteria.** "Assess these three vendors on cost, security, and support" produces a sharper answer than "which vendor is best?"
- **Guard against invention.** Instruct Claude to use only the provided data and to flag where information is missing rather than filling the gap with a plausible-sounding number.

The judgment lesson the exam loves here: fluent analysis can still contain fabricated specifics, and self-reported confidence is not a reliability signal. Verify the load-bearing figures and citations before you act on them, especially for compliance or high-stakes decisions.

## Research

Research tasks ask Claude to find and synthesize information from many sources rather than reason over material you already supplied. In the product this is where research mode — agentic, multi-step web research that returns cited answers — fits. The prompt should steer both *what counts as done* and *how much to trust the findings*.

What to ask for:

- **Define clear success criteria.** Spell out what a complete answer covers, the scope, and any boundaries (timeframe, region, source types). Anthropic's research guidance leads with this.
- **Demand citations and cross-checking.** Ask Claude to cite its sources and to corroborate important claims across more than one, so you can trace and confirm each fact.
- **Ask for structure and transparency on complex questions.** For a big research question, request that Claude break it down systematically, note competing possibilities, and show its confidence, rather than presenting a single tidy answer as settled.

The failure mode is the same as analysis but amplified by reach: a citation that looks authoritative can be fabricated or misrepresented. The professional habit is to open the cited source and confirm it says what Claude claims before you forward the finding — never send an unverified citation to a stakeholder.

## Drafting

Drafting tasks — emails, memos, reports, announcements, documentation — are about producing polished output in a specific voice for a specific reader. Correctness still matters, but the differentiator is *audience fit, tone, and format*.

What to ask for:

- **Name the audience and purpose.** Who reads this, and what should they think, feel, or do afterward? A note meant to reassure a nervous client reads nothing like one meant to prompt a fast decision.
- **Set tone and length explicitly**, matched to the channel — a Slack message, a formal report, and a customer email have different registers and different lengths.
- **Specify the format**, and prefer telling Claude what to do over what to avoid ("write in flowing prose paragraphs" rather than "no bullet points").
- **Show a model when you have one.** A past deliverable that hit the mark is often the fastest way to lock in house style — that is few-shot prompting applied to writing.

Drafting is also where iteration pays off most: get a first version, then refine tone and length through follow-up turns. And it is where data-sensitivity discipline applies — anonymize regulated personal information before uploading source material, rather than relying on an instruction to "not retain" it.

## Brainstorming

Brainstorming inverts the priorities. Here you want *volume, variety, and unexpected angles*, and premature judgment is the enemy. Over-constraining the prompt is the classic mistake.

What to ask for:

- **Ask for quantity and range.** "Give me 20 ideas spanning safe, ambitious, and unconventional" invites the spread you want; "give me the best idea" collapses it too early.
- **Encourage divergence before convergence.** Generate broadly first; rank, filter, and pick in a *separate* later step. Forcing evaluation into the same breath as generation kills the weirder ideas that are often the valuable ones.
- **Vary the vantage point.** Ask Claude to approach the problem as different personas or through different lenses (a skeptic, a customer, a competitor) to break out of the obvious.
- **Keep constraints light and generative.** A little framing helps ("ideas that need no budget"), but heavy rules choke the flow.

The exam-relevant judgment: match the strategy to the phase. Brainstorm with an open, divergent prompt; then switch modes and prompt for convergence — analysis and selection — once you have the raw material.

## Reading the task and switching modes

Real work often blends types, and the skill is sequencing them with the right prompt for each stage. A single project might research the market (cited, verified), brainstorm positioning angles (broad, divergent), analyze which angles fit the product (grounded, criteria-based), then draft the launch memo (audience, tone, format). Each stage deserves its own tailored prompt rather than one generic instruction stretched across all four.

Common pitfalls the exam probes:

- **Prompting a brainstorm like an analysis**, demanding a single justified answer and losing the breadth.
- **Prompting an analysis like a brainstorm**, getting many loose ideas where you needed rigor grounded in the source.
- **Accepting research or analysis output without verifying the citations and figures**, however confident it sounds.
- **Over-constraining creative tasks** so early that the useful, unexpected options never surface.

Recognizing which of the four you are doing — and adjusting grounding, structure, tone, and divergence accordingly — is the core competency this topic tests.
