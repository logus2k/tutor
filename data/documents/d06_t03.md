# Follow Organizational AI Policies and Governance Standards

Using Claude well at work is not only about what the model can do or what Anthropic's policy allows — it is about staying inside *your own organization's* rules. The Foundations exam expects a professional who treats the company AI policy as binding, works only within sanctioned tools and approved data flows, seeks the right approvals, and leaves a trail of accountability. This is the governance layer that sits on top of Anthropic's Usage Policy.

## Two policies always apply at once

Every business use of Claude is governed by two rulebooks simultaneously. The first is **Anthropic's Usage Policy**, which defines what anyone may do with the model. The second is your **organization's AI policy**, which is usually stricter and more specific — it decides which tools are approved, which data may be used with them, who must sign off on which uses, and how usage is recorded. Compliance means satisfying *both*. A task can be perfectly acceptable to Anthropic and still violate your employer's policy (for example, pasting confidential source code into a personal account), and that violation is the one that gets a professional in trouble. On the exam, when a scenario mentions a company rule, that rule wins over personal convenience.

## Sanctioned tools and shadow AI

A recurring governance failure is **shadow AI** — using an unapproved AI tool, or a personal account, for work the company has not sanctioned. Organizations approve specific tools and plans for good reasons: the approved environment carries the right contractual terms (commercial terms, data-processing agreements, a BAA where health data is involved), the right data-retention and training settings, audit logging, and access controls. A personal consumer login has none of that governance wrapped around it.

The exam-relevant rule: do the work in the environment your organization has approved for that class of data, and do not route around it because a personal tool is faster or more familiar. A worked example: your company has an Enterprise Claude workspace with data protections and logging, but you find it quicker to paste a client contract into your own free account at home. The convenient choice is the governance violation — the sanctioned workspace exists precisely so that confidential and regulated work stays inside controlled, auditable boundaries.

## Data-handling rules and classification

Most mature AI policies tie *what data* you may use to *which tool and which approval*. Public and general internal content is usually fine in approved tools; confidential material (contracts, unreleased financials, customer data) may be restricted to specific workspaces; and regulated personal data (PII, PHI, financial records) typically requires anonymization first and a specifically approved, contractually covered environment. Knowing your organization's data classification scheme — and matching each input to its allowed handling — is a core governance competency. When in doubt about whether a given dataset may be used, the correct move is to check the policy or ask, not to proceed and hope.

## Approvals and escalation

Governance frameworks define **who decides**. New or higher-risk uses often need sign-off before they go live — for instance, using Claude in a customer-facing workflow, in a regulated decision (lending, hiring, healthcare), or with a new category of sensitive data. This connects to Anthropic's own high-risk requirements: consequential, consumer-facing uses in legal, financial, medical, and employment contexts require a qualified human in the loop and disclosure that AI was involved. Organizationally, that translates into a named reviewer, a documented approval, and clear ownership of the final decision. The exam's expected behavior is to escalate such cases through the proper channel rather than self-authorize.

## Audit, accountability, and human ownership

Governance depends on being able to answer, after the fact, *who did what and why*. That is why sanctioned enterprise environments provide audit logging and administrative oversight, and why policies assign accountability to a person, not to the model. Claude is a tool; the human who uses its output owns the result. Practically, this means:

- Keep AI use inside logged, approved workspaces so activity is traceable.
- Record approvals and the human reviewer for consequential or high-risk uses.
- Follow disclosure rules — tell downstream users (and, where required, customers) when AI helped produce a decision or communication.
- Never treat "the AI generated it" as a defense; a person is always answerable for the output.

The tempting-but-wrong stance the exam probes is diffusing responsibility onto the tool ("Claude wrote it, so any error is the model's fault"). Accountability never transfers to the model.

## A governance checklist for any AI task

1. **Is this tool/plan sanctioned** by my organization for this kind of work? If not, use the approved one.
2. **Is this data allowed** here under our classification rules, and does it need anonymizing first?
3. **Does this use need approval** (customer-facing, regulated decision, new sensitive data category)? If so, get sign-off before proceeding.
4. **Is it traceable and disclosed** — done in a logged workspace, with the human reviewer and any required AI disclosure in place?
5. **Who owns the output?** Confirm a named human is accountable for verifying and using it.

Running this checklist mirrors exactly the judgment the exam rewards: aligning your usage with company policy, approvals, data-handling rules, sanctioned tools, and accountability — before you start, not after something goes wrong.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- Using a personal or unapproved tool for work data because it is faster (shadow AI).
- Ignoring the company's data-classification rules and uploading confidential or regulated data into a non-approved environment.
- Self-authorizing a high-risk or customer-facing use that policy says requires approval and a human reviewer.
- Skipping disclosure that AI was involved where the policy or Anthropic's high-risk rules require it.
- Treating the model as the accountable party instead of the human using it.
