# Identify Appropriate and Inappropriate Use Cases

Knowing what to hand to Claude — and what to keep away from it — is a core professional judgment the Foundations exam tests directly. The exam rewards candidates who can look at a task and quickly place it in one of three buckets: a good fit, a poor fit that needs a human in the loop, or a use that is outright prohibited by Anthropic's Usage Policy.

## Why this is a judgment skill, not a rule lookup

Claude is a capable general-purpose assistant, but capability is not the same as suitability. A task can be well within Claude's technical reach and still be a bad idea because the stakes are high, the domain is regulated, or the activity is disallowed. The exam framing (the "minimally qualified candidate") assumes a business professional who can reason about fit rather than memorize a checklist. The underlying question is always the same: *what happens if the output is wrong, and who is accountable for it?*

## Good-fit use cases

Claude excels at knowledge work where a human reviews the result before it has consequences. Strong fits include drafting and editing (emails, memos, proposals, job descriptions), summarizing long documents and meetings, brainstorming and reframing ideas, restructuring or classifying information, first-pass data analysis over material you provide, translating between formats, writing and explaining code, and multi-step research with citations. What these share is that a knowledgeable person stays in control: they set the goal, and they verify and own the output.

A useful heuristic is the "competent draft" test. If the ideal outcome is a strong first draft that a professional will check and refine, Claude is a good fit. If the outcome is a final, unreviewed decision that affects someone's rights, money, health, or safety, it is not — at least not without safeguards.

## Poor-fit use cases requiring human judgment or escalation

Between "great fit" and "prohibited" sits a large middle zone that Anthropic calls **high-risk use cases**. These are legitimate but consequential domains where the Usage Policy requires additional safeguards — specifically, a qualified human in the loop and disclosure that AI was involved. Anthropic's list includes legal advice to end users, healthcare diagnosis and treatment, insurance underwriting and claims, financial decisions such as lending and investment advice, employment and hiring decisions, housing eligibility, academic testing and admissions, and automated journalistic content. The policy states that when Claude's output is consumer-facing in these areas, "a qualified professional in that field must review the content or decision prior to dissemination," and the organization must disclose the use of AI at least at the start of each session.

The exam's expected judgment here is escalation, not avoidance. You do not have to refuse to use Claude to help draft a contract clause or summarize a medical study — but you must not let its output become the final legal, medical, or hiring decision. A worked example: asking Claude to *explain* the typical structure of a commercial lease is a good fit; letting Claude *auto-reject* rental applicants or *auto-deny* a loan is exactly the pattern the policy forbids. The tempting-but-wrong instinct is "Claude sounds confident and expert, so I can rely on it directly." Self-reported confidence is not a reliability signal, and confidence in a high-stakes domain is precisely where a human expert must intervene.

Two other poor-fit patterns the exam probes: relying on Claude for facts you cannot verify (models can fabricate specific-looking citations, statistics, and subsection numbers — always verify against an authoritative source before sending to a compliance or executive audience), and treating Claude as a system of record or decision-maker rather than an assistant to a decision-maker.

## Prohibited use cases

Some uses are off-limits regardless of safeguards. Anthropic's Usage Policy sets universal standards that no user may cross. In plain terms, do not use Claude to:

- Generate child sexual abuse material or endanger minors in any way.
- Develop weapons, explosives, or chemical, biological, nuclear, or radiological materials.
- Compromise computers or networks — malware, exploits, denial-of-service, or circumventing security controls without authorization.
- Disrupt critical infrastructure (power, water, medical devices, voting systems).
- Run fraud, phishing, scams, spam, counterfeiting, or fake reviews.
- Produce disinformation, impersonate real people, or run deceptive influence and voter-targeting operations that undermine democratic processes.
- Conduct unlawful surveillance, biometric tracking, facial recognition, or social scoring that violates civil liberties.
- Collect or misuse personal data without consent, or use Claude for harassment, incitement, or violence.

These categories are worth recognizing on sight, because the exam may present a scenario that looks like an ordinary business task ("write persuasive messages to voters," "generate product reviews," "help me access this account") but is actually a prohibited activity in disguise.

## A quick decision rule

When you evaluate a task, ask in order: (1) Is it prohibited by the Usage Policy? If yes, stop. (2) Is it a high-risk domain with consequences for a real person? If yes, require a qualified human reviewer and disclose the AI's involvement. (3) Otherwise, is it knowledge work whose output I will verify before it matters? If yes, it is a good fit — proceed, then check the result. This three-step screen mirrors how the exam expects you to reason, and it keeps you on the right side of both policy and good practice.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- Assuming "Claude can do it" means "Claude should do it." Capability never overrides policy or stakes.
- Skipping the human reviewer in legal, medical, financial, or hiring contexts because the draft looks polished.
- Trusting confident-sounding facts and citations without verification.
- Missing that a friendly-looking prompt (mass voter messaging, fake reviews, credential access) maps to a prohibited category.
