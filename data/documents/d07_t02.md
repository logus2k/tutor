# Adjust Approach Based on Feedback and Results

Getting good work out of Claude is rarely a single perfect prompt. It is a loop: you prompt, you read the result critically, and you *change your approach* based on what came back. The exam tests whether you can read an output's shortcomings and pick the right next move — re-prompt, decompose, add knowledge, change model tier, or escalate to a human — rather than repeating the same request and hoping.

## Adopt a feedback-loop mindset

Treat every output as diagnostic information, not a verdict. A weak first draft tells you something specific: what context was missing, which instruction was ambiguous, which part of the task was too big. The skilled user closes that gap on the next turn. The core habit is: **observe → interpret → adjust → re-run**, and keep the changes small and deliberate so you can tell what actually helped.

This matters because the tempting wrong move — clicking "regenerate" on an unchanged prompt — treats a systematic problem as random noise. If the prompt was ambiguous, every regeneration will be ambiguous. Change an input, then judge the output.

## Read the output critically first

Before you act, decide *what kind* of gap you are looking at, because different gaps call for different adjustments:

- Is it **underspecified** (right topic, wrong depth/tone/format)? → the fix lives in your instructions.
- Is it **ungrounded** (Claude didn't have the facts and may have guessed)? → the fix is adding knowledge.
- Is it **too large** (partial, rushed, or internally inconsistent)? → the fix is decomposition.
- Is it **beyond the setup** (genuinely hard reasoning, or genuinely needs a person)? → the fix is a stronger model tier or human escalation.

Naming the gap prevents the common error of applying the wrong remedy — for example, jumping to a more powerful model when the real problem was that you never told Claude the audience.

## The adjustment moves

**Re-prompt with more specificity.** The highest-yield first move. Feed your critique back in: "That's too formal — rewrite for a friendly internal audience at half the length," or "Good structure, but add a risks section and cite the source clause for each point." Iterative refinement — using the previous output as the starting point for a sharper instruction — steadily converges on what you want and is often all you need.

**Decompose the task.** If one prompt tried to research, analyze, and draft all at once and did all three poorly, split it. Ask Claude to first extract the key facts, confirm those are right, then analyze, then draft. Breaking a complex job into a chain of smaller steps — sometimes called prompt chaining — improves quality and gives you checkpoints to catch errors early, before they compound into the final deliverable.

**Add knowledge.** When outputs are vague or fabricated because Claude is working from general knowledge, ground it: attach the relevant document, paste the data, or point a connector at the source (for example, Google Drive or Gmail with permission). In claude.ai, uploading your style guide, past reports, or reference material — or storing them in a Project's knowledge base — turns generic answers into ones anchored in your actual material. Pair this with "use only the provided sources" to keep Claude from drifting back to guesses.

**Switch model tier.** If, after clarifying and grounding, the task still exceeds the model's reasoning — genuinely intricate multi-step analysis, long-horizon work — step up to a more capable tier (Sonnet, or Opus for the hardest reasoning). Conversely, if a simple, high-volume task is being handled by an overpowered model, step *down* to Haiku for speed and cost. The judgment cuts both ways: escalate for capability, downgrade for efficiency. Crucially, reach for a tier change *after* you have ruled out prompt and grounding problems, since most disappointing outputs are instruction problems, not capability ceilings.

**Escalate to a human.** Some results should trigger a person, not another prompt. If the task involves a legal, medical, financial, or compliance judgment; if Claude expresses genuine uncertainty on a high-stakes point; or if verifying the output would require expertise or authority you don't have — route it to the right specialist. Recognizing the limit of what you should decide from an AI output is itself a tested competency.

## A worked example

You ask Claude to "analyze our customer churn and recommend fixes." The first output is a generic list of churn-reduction tips any consultant could have written. Reading it critically, the gap is *ungrounded and underspecified*, not a model weakness. Adjustment sequence: (1) attach the actual churn data and last quarter's support tickets; (2) re-prompt for analysis tied to *that* data with the specific customer segment and time window named; (3) decompose — first ask for the top three churn drivers evidenced in the data, confirm they look right, then ask for recommendations against each. If the causal analysis is still shaky, *then* consider a higher tier. And before these recommendations reach leadership, a human validates the numbers. Notice what you did *not* do first: swap models. That would have been the tempting-but-wrong reflex, spending cost and latency to paper over a missing-context problem.

## Pitfalls the exam probes

- **Regenerating without changing anything.** Same input, same class of output.
- **Changing several things at once.** You learn nothing about what worked; adjust deliberately.
- **Reaching for a bigger model first.** Clarify and ground before assuming a capability limit — and remember the reverse, that a top-tier model on trivial work wastes budget.
- **Never escalating.** Treating every result as final, even when the stakes clearly call for human sign-off, is a failure of judgment the exam penalizes.

The unifying idea: outputs are feedback. Read them, name the gap, choose the matching adjustment, and iterate — with a human in the loop wherever the stakes demand it.
