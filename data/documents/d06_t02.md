# Apply Data Sensitivity, Regulatory, and Privacy Considerations

The single most reliable "wrong answer" trap on the Foundations exam is the one where a well-meaning professional uploads regulated personal data to an AI tool and tries to manage the risk after the fact. This topic is about the opposite instinct: classify the sensitivity of your data and apply the right controls *before* the data ever reaches Claude. The exam's Sample Q3 lesson makes the rule concrete — anonymize regulated data before use.

## Classify sensitivity before you act

Every input you consider pasting or uploading falls somewhere on a sensitivity scale, and the level dictates the handling:

- **Public** — already published or freely shareable (marketing copy, public filings). Low concern.
- **Internal** — non-public but low-harm business content (internal drafts, general process notes). Handle per company policy.
- **Confidential** — trade secrets, unreleased financials, contracts, source code, customer lists. Real business harm if leaked.
- **Regulated / highly sensitive** — data governed by law: personal data (PII), protected health information (PHI), financial account data, and similar. This is the category that triggers legal obligations, not just preferences.

The exam expects you to *notice* when data crosses into the regulated tier, because that is where a casual habit becomes a compliance violation.

## What counts as regulated personal data

**PII (personally identifiable information):** names combined with identifiers such as national ID or social security numbers, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, dates of birth, account numbers, and government-issued IDs. Governed by laws like the GDPR and various privacy statutes.

**PHI (protected health information):** any health data tied to an identifiable person — diagnoses, treatment records, lab results, insurance claims. Governed in the US by HIPAA.

**Financial data:** payment card numbers, bank account and routing numbers, and other regulated financial records (PCI DSS, GLBA, and similar regimes).

The common thread is that a real, identifiable individual can be harmed and a specific law assigns you responsibility for protecting the data. Sensitivity often comes from *combination*: a diagnosis alone may be innocuous, but a diagnosis next to a name and date of birth is PHI.

## Redact or anonymize BEFORE uploading

The core rule: strip or mask regulated identifiers *before* the data enters the tool. If you need Claude to summarize patient feedback, replace names and identifiers with placeholders (Patient A, Patient B) and remove anything that could re-identify someone. If you need a financial analysis, remove account numbers and use aggregates. The goal is that the material you upload no longer maps to a specific individual.

A worked example straight from the exam's logic: you are asked to have Claude analyze a spreadsheet of customer loan applications. The right move is to remove names, SSNs, and account numbers — anonymizing the records — before uploading, then run the analysis on the de-identified data. The tempting-but-wrong move is to upload the raw file and add an instruction like "please don't store or remember this." That is not a control.

## Why "tell Claude not to retain it" is not a control

An instruction inside a prompt is a request to the model, not an enforceable data-handling guarantee. It does not change how the platform processes, logs, or retains data; it does not satisfy a regulator; and it does not undo the fact that the regulated data was disclosed to a third-party system. Compliance controls are governance and platform settings — data classification, approved tools, contractual terms, retention configuration, access controls — not sentences typed into a chat box. The exam draws a hard line here: a natural-language plea to the model can never substitute for anonymizing the data first or using an appropriately contracted environment.

## Match the environment to the data

Not every Claude surface is appropriate for every data class. Anthropic distinguishes consumer plans from commercial ones. For consumer Claude, conversations are **not** used to train models by default; users may *opt in* to share chats for model improvement, which extends retention to as long as five years for new or resumed sessions, while the default retention is 30 days. Commercial offerings (Claude for Work — Team and Enterprise — and the API under Anthropic's Commercial Terms) are governed separately: those inputs and outputs are not used for training, and enterprise data handling is contractually locked down.

For regulated health data specifically, Anthropic offers **HIPAA-ready** configurations and will sign a **Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** for eligible Enterprise plans and the API — but *not* for Free, Pro, Max, Team, or self-serve tiers. The exam-relevant takeaway is not the fine print but the principle: putting PHI into a plan with no BAA is a governance failure even if the model performs the task perfectly. Sensitive data belongs only in a sanctioned environment approved for it.

## A decision rule you can apply on the exam

1. **Classify** the data. Is any of it regulated personal data (PII/PHI/financial)?
2. If yes, **minimize and anonymize** — remove or mask identifiers so records no longer point to an individual; upload only what the task actually needs.
3. **Match the environment** — use the tool/plan your organization has approved for that data class (e.g., a BAA-covered Enterprise plan for PHI), never a personal consumer account for regulated work.
4. Only then use Claude — and remember that an in-prompt "don't keep this" is never step 2 or 3.

## Common pitfalls

- Treating a prompt instruction ("don't retain this") as a substitute for redaction. It is not.
- Uploading raw regulated files because the analysis "needs the real data" — anonymized data almost always suffices.
- Ignoring the combination effect: individually harmless fields become PII/PHI when joined to an identifier.
- Using a personal or unapproved plan for regulated data instead of the sanctioned, contractually covered environment.
