# Use Claude to Support Solution Design, Development, and Iteration

Once requirements are clear, Claude helps you move from idea to a working deliverable — a document, a template, a dashboard mock-up, a small interactive tool — and then refine it through fast cycles of feedback. This topic tests whether you can drive that build-and-iterate loop deliberately, using the right features, rather than accepting the first draft.

## Drafting the first solution

The fastest way to a good deliverable is a good first draft to react to. Claude produces one quickly when you give it four things: a **role** ("you are a UX writer"), the **goal**, the **constraints** (audience, length, tone, format, what to avoid), and an **example or template** if you have one. "Draft a one-page project brief for a website redesign; audience is non-technical executives; under 400 words; include goals, scope, timeline, and risks" yields something you can immediately critique.

A first draft is a starting point, not the deliverable. The exam favors the practitioner who treats Claude's output as raw material to be verified and shaped, not as a finished answer. Any factual claim in the draft — a statistic, a date, a cited source — is unverified until you check it, because models can produce specific-looking details that are wrong.

## Prototyping with Artifacts

**Artifacts** are Claude's mechanism for standalone, editable content shown in a side panel next to the conversation: documents, tables, diagrams, charts, and small interactive mini-apps or tools. They are the workhorse for prototyping because you can *see* the result and *change it in place* instead of copying text back and forth.

What this enables for a business user:

- **Tangible drafts.** Ask for a Kanban board, a Gantt-style timeline, an ROI calculator, or a KPI dashboard mock-up and get an interactive artifact you can click, not just a description of one.
- **Preview before committing.** Draft an email sequence or a landing-page layout as an artifact and review how it actually reads or looks before it goes anywhere real.
- **In-place iteration.** Because the artifact updates as you give feedback, you refine "make it shorter, add a status column, change the tone to warmer" and watch it change, keeping one clean version instead of a dozen pasted copies.
- **Reuse and share.** A useful artifact — a calculator, a checklist, a template — can be shared with teammates so the whole team works from the same asset.

Artifacts are ideal for *prototyping and internal deliverables*. They are not a substitute for your production systems of record, and anything data-driven inside an artifact still needs its numbers verified before you rely on it.

## Iterating toward the deliverable

Iteration is where Claude adds the most value, and doing it well is a skill the exam probes. Effective iteration is *specific and one change at a time*.

- **Give targeted feedback.** "Tighten paragraph two, cut the jargon, and make the call-to-action more direct" beats "make it better." Vague feedback produces a random rewrite; specific feedback converges.
- **Iterate in small steps.** Change one dimension per pass so you can tell what improved. Sweeping "redo everything" prompts make it hard to keep the parts that were already good.
- **Have Claude critique its own work.** "Review this draft as a skeptical stakeholder and list its three weakest points" surfaces problems before a real reviewer does — but treat the critique as input, not gospel.
- **Preserve good versions.** When a version is close, save it before the next change so an experiment can't lose progress.
- **Anchor with examples.** Pasting a "good" example and a "bad" example teaches Claude your standard faster than adjectives do.

Use a **Project** to hold the deliverable, its source material, and a custom instruction ("our brand voice is plain, confident, no hype") so every iteration stays consistent and a teammate can pick up where you left off. **Memory** can carry preferences across conversations so you re-state them less, though you should still confirm important constraints rather than assume they carried over.

### Matching the model tier to the work

Match the tier to the difficulty of the design task. **Sonnet**, the balanced workhorse, is the sensible default for most drafting and iteration. **Haiku** suits quick, high-volume, simple passes. **Opus** is worth it for genuinely hard design reasoning or complex, multi-part deliverables. Defaulting to the top tier for a simple rewrite wastes cost and speed; that "match the model to the task" judgment is exactly what the certification tests.

## A worked mini-example

Goal: a reusable client-onboarding checklist as a shareable tool.

1. **Draft** — Ask Claude (in a Project holding your existing onboarding notes) for a checklist covering kickoff, access, and first-week milestones.
2. **Prototype** — Have it build the checklist as an interactive Artifact with checkboxes and owners.
3. **Iterate** — "Add a 'blocked' status, group by week, and add a notes column," reviewing each change in the panel.
4. **Verify** — Confirm the steps match your real process and that any referenced dates or tools are correct — Claude may have inferred a plausible but wrong step.
5. **Share** — Publish the artifact for the team and set it as the standard.

Notice the loop: draft, make tangible, refine narrowly, *verify*, ship. The verify step is not optional polish; it's where you catch the fabricated or assumed detail before it reaches a client.

## Common pitfalls the exam probes

- **Shipping the first draft** without iteration or a verification pass.
- **Vague iteration** ("make it better") that produces churn instead of convergence.
- **Trusting numbers or facts inside an artifact** because they're formatted nicely.
- **Treating an artifact prototype as a production system** rather than a draft to validate.
- **Over-powering the task** with the top model tier when the workhorse tier would do.

The through-line: Claude accelerates going from blank page to a polished, tangible, iteratively refined deliverable — provided you stay the editor and verifier, not just the requester.
