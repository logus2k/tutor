# Align Model Selection With Task Requirements (Cost, Speed, Quality)

Knowing the Claude tiers exists is only half the skill. The exam wants you to *decide* — to look at a real task and pick the tier that fits its actual requirements. This is a business-judgment call balancing three levers: **cost, speed, and quality.** The single most tested lesson in this domain is that the best model is the one that matches the task, not the most powerful one you can access.

## The three levers you are trading off

Every task pulls on three constraints at once:

- **Cost** — you pay per unit of work. On high-volume tasks, small per-item differences multiply into large bills.
- **Speed (latency)** — how long each response takes. When a person or a downstream process is waiting, latency is part of the user experience, not just an efficiency metric.
- **Quality** — how good, deep, and reliable the output needs to be for its purpose.

You cannot maximize all three. More capability (quality) generally means more cost and more latency. The art is deciding how much quality the task genuinely requires, then buying exactly that much — no more.

## The decision, in plain terms

Move up the tiers (Haiku → Sonnet → Opus) only as far as the task's difficulty demands:

1. **How hard is the reasoning, really?** Simple, well-defined, repetitive → a lower tier suffices. Novel, multi-step, high-stakes → a higher tier earns its cost.
2. **How much volume, and how sensitive to latency?** Thousands of items or a user waiting live → favor Haiku's speed and low cost. A handful of careful outputs → you can afford Opus.
3. **What is the cost of a wrong or mediocre answer?** Low stakes (an internal draft, a first pass) → don't overpay. High stakes (a client deliverable, a compliance summary) → prioritize quality.

A practical habit: **start with Sonnet** as the default. If the output is not good enough, step up to Opus. If quality is fine but you need it cheaper or faster at volume, step down to Haiku. This "start in the middle and adjust" approach beats guessing at the extremes.

## The core exam lesson (Sample Q2 pattern)

The exam's signature scenario runs like this: *You need to process a very large volume of straightforward items — for example, categorizing thousands of incoming support messages by topic. Which model should you use?*

The correct reasoning: this is **simple, high-volume, latency-sensitive** work, so the **faster, cheaper tier (Haiku)** is the right match. It handles classification well, and at that volume the cost and speed savings are enormous.

The tempting-but-wrong answer: **"Always use the most capable model (Opus) — it will give the best results."** This is wrong on the exam and in practice, for three reasons:

- **It wastes cost.** Paying top-tier prices per item across thousands of items burns budget with no benefit the task can use — categorization does not need frontier reasoning.
- **It wastes latency budget.** Opus is slower; on high-volume or real-time work, that slowness compounds and degrades the experience.
- **The quality gain is invisible here.** A simple classification is something the cheaper tier already does reliably. The extra capability produces no meaningfully better outcome, so you paid more for nothing.

"Best model" is not the same as "best choice." The best *choice* is the cheapest, fastest tier that still meets the quality bar the task sets.

## The mirror-image case: don't under-provision either

The rule cuts both ways. Reserving the top tier for genuinely hard work means you *should* use Opus when the task warrants it. If you push a complex, multi-step analysis or a long-horizon agentic workflow onto Haiku just to save money, you get fast, cheap output that is not good enough to use — false economy. Matching the model to the task means stepping **up** as readily as stepping **down**. The failure mode the exam punishes is *ignoring the task's real requirements* in either direction.

## Worked mini-examples

- **Routing 10,000 emails to the right department.** High volume, simple decision, latency matters. → **Haiku.** Opus would be slow and expensive for no gain.
- **Drafting a weekly customer newsletter in a set brand voice.** Moderate complexity, quality matters, modest volume. → **Sonnet.** Reliable quality without overpaying.
- **Producing a detailed strategic analysis synthesizing many documents, feeding a board decision.** Hard reasoning, high stakes, low volume. → **Opus.** The quality justifies the cost and time.
- **A quick internal summary of one meeting transcript.** Low stakes, one-off. → **Sonnet or even Haiku.** No need for Opus.

## A simple selection checklist

Before choosing, ask: How complex is the reasoning? How many items / how sensitive to latency? How costly is a mediocre answer? Then pick the **lowest tier that clears the quality bar** the task sets — and be ready to move up if it doesn't.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- **"Always use the best model."** The headline wrong answer. It ignores cost and speed and treats capability as free.
- **Choosing on cost alone.** Under-provisioning a genuinely hard task to save money produces unusable output — also wrong.
- **Ignoring latency.** Cost is not the only reason to go cheaper; on real-time or high-volume work, speed is a first-class requirement.
- **Not reconsidering after a poor result.** If Sonnet's answer falls short on a hard task, the fix may be Opus (or a better prompt) — model selection is iterative, not a one-time guess.
