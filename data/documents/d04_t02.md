# Leverage Claude for Research, Planning, and Process Optimization

Knowledge workers spend a large share of their time gathering information, planning work, and looking for ways to make repeatable processes faster. This topic tests whether you can direct Claude — including its agentic research mode — to do that gathering and planning well, and, critically, whether you verify what it returns before acting on it.

## Research: using research mode well

Claude's **research mode** is agentic: instead of a single search, it plans a line of inquiry, runs multiple searches that build on each other, decides what to investigate next, and returns a synthesized answer with **inline citations you can click to verify**. When connectors are enabled, it can research across both the public web and your internal context (for example Gmail, Google Calendar, and Google Docs), so a single query can pull together external market data and your own meeting notes. Research is available on paid plans and is turned on from the "+" menu in the chat; a common failure is expecting deep research from a plain chat — if Claude doesn't do it automatically, explicitly ask it to "use the research tool."

Good practice with research mode:

- **Scope the question.** "Research the top three project-management tools for a 20-person agency, comparing price, integrations, and onboarding effort, and cite each claim" beats "tell me about PM tools." A scoped question produces checkable, comparable output.
- **Ask for citations and use them.** The value of research mode is the citation trail. Because Claude can fabricate specific-looking details, the citation is what lets you confirm a stated price or statistic is real. Treat an uncited number as unverified.
- **Mind the cost.** Research retrieves many sources and consumes usage limits faster than a normal chat. Reserve it for genuinely multi-source questions; use ordinary chat for quick lookups.

The tempting wrong move is to paste research output straight into a client deck. The disciplined move is to spot-check the load-bearing claims against the cited sources first — especially for regulated, financial, or executive audiences where a single fabricated figure is costly. Self-reported confidence from Claude is not evidence.

## Matching the model tier to research work

Research and planning tasks vary in difficulty, and the exam tests matching the model tier to the task rather than always reaching for the most powerful one.

- **Haiku** — fastest and cheapest; good for simple, high-volume, latency-sensitive steps such as classifying incoming items or quick lookups.
- **Sonnet** — the balanced workhorse and the sensible default for most research synthesis and planning.
- **Opus** — most capable for the hardest, long-horizon reasoning and complex multi-step analysis.

Always defaulting to the top tier wastes cost and latency budget on work a faster tier handles well; picking a tier below the task's complexity risks shallow analysis. Choose deliberately.

## Planning: turning goals into a workable plan

Claude is strong at converting an objective into a structured plan you can critique. Effective prompts give it a role, the goal, the constraints, and the output shape: "You are a program manager. Draft a 6-week plan to migrate our team to a new ticketing system. Assume 4 people at 20% time. Produce phases, milestones, dependencies, risks, and an owner column."

High-value planning patterns:

- **Work backward from the deadline.** Ask Claude to start from the target date and derive what must be true each week. This surfaces schedule conflicts early.
- **Pressure-test the plan.** "Play devil's advocate: where is this plan most likely to slip, and what's the single biggest risk I'm underweighting?" A second pass as a skeptic catches optimism that the first draft baked in.
- **Generate options, not just an answer.** For a decision, ask for two or three approaches with explicit tradeoffs rather than one recommendation. You stay the decision-maker; Claude widens the option set.
- **Iterate in a Project.** For multi-week planning, keep the plan and its source documents in a Project so context persists and teammates work from the same version instead of re-explaining the situation each session.

Remember that Claude does not know your unstated constraints — budget ceilings, politics, a fixed vendor. State them, or the plan will be plausible but inapplicable.

## Process optimization: finding improvements

Claude is a useful lens for examining a repeatable process. Describe the current workflow step by step — or paste an SOP — and ask it to find friction: "Here's how we handle refund requests. Map each step, flag steps that are manual, redundant, or a likely bottleneck, and suggest where a template or automation would help most."

Techniques that work:

- **Map before you optimize.** Have Claude restate the current process as numbered steps and confirm it's accurate before proposing changes. Optimizing a misunderstood process produces wrong advice.
- **Quantify where you can.** Ask which steps consume the most time or cause the most rework; prioritize there rather than polishing trivial steps.
- **Draft the reusable asset.** When a step is "write roughly the same email every time," have Claude produce a reusable template or a Project instruction so the improvement sticks. Repeatable, well-defined steps are the best automation candidates.
- **Keep the human where judgment lives.** A refund *policy exception* or a *tone-sensitive* customer reply should stay with a person; the routine drafting and triage around it are what you streamline.

### A worked mini-example

A marketing team spends hours each Monday compiling a performance summary. Mapped with Claude, the process is: pull numbers (manual, from a dashboard), write commentary (repetitive), format the email (repetitive). Claude can't reach the live dashboard, so that step stays human — but once the numbers are pasted in, a Project with a fixed instruction turns commentary-plus-formatting from an hour into minutes. The optimization insight is to automate the *language-heavy, repeatable* portion and keep the *data-access and judgment* portions where they belong. That discipline — improve the repeatable middle, verify the facts, keep humans on judgment — is what this topic rewards.

## Common pitfalls

- Trusting research output without opening the citations.
- Using deep research (and burning limits) for a question a quick chat would answer.
- Asking Claude to optimize a process it never accurately mapped.
- Defaulting to the top model tier for routine research and planning.
