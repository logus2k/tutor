# Evaluate Claude-Generated Outputs for Accuracy and Completeness

Output Evaluation and Validation is the heaviest domain on the Claude Certified Associate – Foundations exam, and this is its foundation: before you use, forward, or act on anything Claude produces, you check whether it is both *correct* and *complete*. The exam rewards the habit of treating a Claude response as a draft to be verified, not an answer to be trusted.

## Two separate questions: accuracy and completeness

A response can be accurate but incomplete, or complete but wrong. Strong evaluation separates the two.

**Accuracy** asks: is what Claude said actually true and internally consistent? Are the facts, figures, names, dates, and quotes correct? Do the conclusions follow from the stated evidence?

**Completeness** asks: did Claude answer the *whole* request? A prompt like "Summarize this contract and flag any termination and liability clauses" contains three deliverables — a summary, termination clauses, and liability clauses. A polished summary that silently ignores liability is a failure, even if every word of the summary is true. The most common evaluation miss is not a false statement; it is a quietly dropped part of the ask.

## A practical evaluation routine

1. **Restate the ask as a checklist.** List every explicit deliverable and every implicit constraint (audience, length, format, tone, scope). Then confirm the output satisfies each item. This is the single most reliable way to catch coverage gaps.
2. **Trace claims to evidence.** For anything factual, ask where it came from. If Claude worked from a document you provided, the claim should be traceable to that document. If it relied on general knowledge, treat specifics (statistics, citations, quotes, section numbers) as unverified until checked.
3. **Spot-check the specifics.** Numbers, proper nouns, dates, and citations are where errors hide. Verify a sample; if the sample is shaky, widen the check.
4. **Read for internal consistency.** Does paragraph four contradict paragraph one? Does a total match its line items? Contradictions inside a single response are a strong signal that some part was fabricated.
5. **Judge fitness for the audience and purpose.** An output can be accurate and complete yet still unfit — too technical for the intended reader, or missing the "so what." Completeness includes answering the *underlying* question, not just the literal words.

## Worked mini-example

You ask Claude to "compare our three vendor proposals on price, implementation timeline, and data-security posture, and recommend one." A response that produces a clean recommendation but only discusses price and timeline is **incomplete** — the security dimension, which may be decisive, is missing. A response that covers all three but states "Vendor B is SOC 2 Type II certified" when the proposal never said so is **inaccurate** — a fabricated specific. Good evaluation flags both before the recommendation reaches a stakeholder.

## Techniques that make outputs easier to evaluate

You can design prompts so that verification is cheaper. Anthropic's own guidance on reducing hallucinations recommends grounding responses in source material and making them auditable:

- **Ask for sources or quotes.** Have Claude cite the exact passage that supports each claim. Claims without support are the ones to scrutinize or drop.
- **Give permission to say "I don't know."** Explicitly allowing Claude to admit uncertainty reduces confident-but-false filler and makes gaps visible instead of papered over.
- **Restrict to provided knowledge.** For document-based tasks, instruct Claude to use only the supplied material, so any claim can be checked against it.
- **Ask Claude to self-check coverage.** "List each part of my request and confirm you addressed it" turns the completeness check into part of the output — though you still verify it yourself, because a self-report is a claim, not proof.

## Define what "good" means before you judge

Anthropic's evaluation guidance stresses defining success criteria that are *specific, measurable, achievable, and relevant*. For a business user this means deciding, ideally before you prompt, what a passing answer must contain: which questions it must resolve, which sources it must respect, what format it must take. Vague standards ("make it good") produce vague evaluation. Concrete criteria ("covers all four risk categories, cites the policy section for each, fits on one page") let you judge accuracy and completeness objectively and consistently.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- **Fluency mistaken for correctness.** A confident, well-organized, professionally worded answer feels authoritative. Polish is not evidence. The exam repeatedly contrasts a tempting "it reads well, ship it" choice with the correct "verify the specifics first."
- **Confidence treated as reliability.** If Claude says it is "certain," that is not a validation signal. Self-reported confidence does not correlate with truth; verify anyway.
- **Checking accuracy but forgetting completeness.** Evaluators often fact-check individual sentences while missing that a whole requested deliverable is absent. Always reconcile the output against the full ask.
- **Accepting a plausible summary of a long document.** With long inputs, Claude can smooth over or invent details. Grounding in quotes and spot-checking against the source is the correct response, not trusting the tidy summary.

**The rule to remember:** evaluate every output on two axes — is it *true* and is it *whole*? Confirm correctness by tracing and spot-checking specifics, and confirm coverage by reconciling against a checklist of the original request. When either axis is unverified, the output is a draft, not a deliverable.
