# Identify, Diagnose, and Resolve Issues with Underperforming Prompts or Poor Outputs

When Claude returns something unhelpful, the exam expects you to respond like a professional troubleshooter: name the failure mode, trace it to a root cause in your prompt or setup, and apply a *targeted* fix — not to reroll blindly or assume the model is simply "wrong." A messy first output is usually a signal about the instructions it was given.

## Recognize the common failure modes

Most poor outputs fall into a handful of recognizable categories. Learning to name them is half the fix.

- **Vague or generic.** The answer is technically on-topic but shallow, hedged, or could apply to any company. It reads like filler.
- **Incomplete.** Claude stops early, covers only part of a multi-part request, or promises to "continue later" instead of finishing.
- **Wrong format.** You wanted a five-row table and got three paragraphs; you wanted a JSON-like structure or a specific email layout and got prose.
- **Hallucinated.** Claude invents specific-looking but false details — a citation, a statute subsection, a statistic, a product feature. These are dangerous precisely because they look authoritative.
- **Off-tone or off-audience.** The content is correct but too casual for a board memo, too jargon-heavy for customers, or the wrong length.

A single output can exhibit several at once (e.g., a confidently hallucinated statistic delivered in the wrong tone). Diagnose each separately.

## Diagnose the root cause before you fix

The discipline the exam rewards is pausing to ask *why* the output came out the way it did. Re-read your own prompt as if you were a brilliant new hire with zero context — because that is effectively Claude's position. Ask:

1. **Did I actually state what I wanted?** Vague and off-tone outputs almost always trace back to unstated expectations: no audience named, no length, no format, no role, no success criteria.
2. **Did Claude have the information it needed?** Hallucinations most often appear when the model lacks grounding but is still pushed to produce a complete, confident answer. If you asked about your Q3 numbers without giving Claude your Q3 numbers, it may fill the gap by inventing plausible ones.
3. **Was the request too big for one shot?** Incomplete answers frequently mean the task bundled five sub-tasks into one instruction with no structure.
4. **Did I show an example of "good"?** If format keeps drifting, the fix is usually a concrete sample, not louder instructions.

A key judgment point: **Claude's confident tone is not a reliability signal.** A fluent, assertive answer is exactly as likely to be fabricated as a hesitant one. Never treat self-assurance as verification.

## Apply the targeted fix

Match the remedy to the diagnosis rather than reaching for the same lever every time.

**For vague or generic output — be clear, direct, and detailed.** Spell out the audience, purpose, desired length, and what a good answer must include. "Write something about our new plan" becomes "Write a 150-word announcement for existing enterprise customers explaining our new usage-based pricing, emphasizing that no one's bill increases before January." Directness matters: if you want Claude to produce the finished thing, say "Write the email," not "Can you suggest some ideas?"

**For wrong format — show, don't just tell.** Provide one or two worked examples of the exact structure you want (this is few-shot prompting), or paste a template to fill in. Examples dramatically improve consistency and are more reliable than adjectives like "make it professional."

**For incomplete output — decompose and structure.** Break the request into explicit numbered steps, or separate reasoning from the final answer by asking Claude to think through the problem first and then produce the deliverable. Chain-of-thought ("work through this step by step before answering") both improves quality on complex tasks and lets you see where reasoning went off the rails.

**For hallucinations — ground and verify.** Several complementary moves:
- **Give Claude permission to be uncertain.** Add "If the document doesn't contain the answer, say 'I don't have enough information' rather than guessing." This one instruction sharply reduces fabrication.
- **Provide the source material** and restrict Claude to it: "Use only the attached policy; do not rely on general knowledge."
- **Require citations or quotes.** Ask Claude to support each claim with a direct quote from the provided text and to drop any claim it can't support.
- **Verify externally.** For compliance, legal, medical, or financial content, confirm every specific figure, name, and citation against an authoritative source before it leaves your hands. This is a human responsibility that no prompt fully removes.

**For off-tone — set a role and constraints.** "You are a corporate communications lead writing for a risk-averse legal audience; keep it formal and under 200 words." A role in your instructions focuses tone and vocabulary immediately.

## A worked example

You ask, "Summarize this vendor contract's risks," and get four confident bullet points, one citing "Section 12.4's termination penalty." The contract has no Section 12.4. Diagnosis: hallucination from thin grounding plus no permission to admit gaps. Fix: re-prompt with the full contract attached, add "Quote the exact clause for each risk you list; if a risk isn't supported by a specific clause, omit it or flag it as your inference," and then personally verify the flagged clauses. Notice the wrong-but-tempting move — simply regenerating and hoping for a cleaner answer — leaves the root cause (no grounding, no uncertainty license) untouched, so the next output can hallucinate a different fake section.

## Pitfalls the exam probes

- **Rerolling instead of diagnosing.** Regenerating an unchanged prompt is a lottery, not a fix.
- **Blaming the model tier first.** Underperformance is more often a prompt problem than a capability ceiling; clarify the prompt before assuming you need a bigger model.
- **Trusting fluency.** Polished prose is not evidence of accuracy.
- **Skipping verification on high-stakes output.** For regulated or externally published content, the fix always ends in human review.

The throughline: observe the specific failure, trace it to a specific cause, and change the one thing that addresses it.
