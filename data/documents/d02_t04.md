# Determine When Human Review or Additional Verification Is Required

Not every Claude output needs the same scrutiny. The skill the exam tests here is *risk-based triage*: recognizing which outputs you can act on with a light check and which ones require a qualified human to review before anything is finalized or sent. Getting this judgment right is central to using Claude responsibly in a business setting.

## The core principle: match verification to stakes

Verification effort should scale with the consequences of being wrong. A brainstormed list of blog-title ideas carries almost no risk if one is weak — quick self-review is enough. A benefits-eligibility determination, a contract clause, or a medical instruction carries serious risk if it is wrong — it demands human review by someone qualified, plus independent verification of the facts.

Anthropic's usage policy makes this concrete. For high-risk uses — including legal, financial, and employment decisions that affect people — it calls for additional safeguards such as **human-in-the-loop oversight**. Its guidance is that when Claude is used to provide advice, recommendations, or subjective decisions that directly affect individuals or consumers, **a qualified professional in that field must review the content or decision before it is disseminated or finalized**. And more broadly: keep a human in the loop for any consequential or irreversible decision, and treat model output as an input to your reasoning, not a replacement for it.

## Escalation triggers: when to require human review

Route an output to human review (and deeper verification) when any of these apply:

- **High-stakes or irreversible impact.** The decision affects someone's money, employment, health, legal standing, safety, or rights, and cannot be easily undone.
- **Regulated or compliance-bound domains.** Legal, medical, financial, tax, insurance, hiring, lending, housing — areas with professional standards or legal duties. Here the reviewer should be a qualified professional, not just any colleague.
- **Customer-facing or public output.** Anything published, sent to a client, or attributed to your organization, where an error becomes a reputational or contractual liability.
- **Fabrication-prone specifics.** The output hinges on citations, statutes, subsection numbers, statistics, or quotes — exactly the details models most often invent.
- **Novel, ambiguous, or low-context situations.** Claude is extrapolating beyond the material you gave it, or the request is underspecified.
- **Bias-sensitive content.** Decisions or descriptions touching protected characteristics, or that could unfairly disadvantage a group.
- **Detected instability.** Regenerations disagree, the output contradicts itself, or it conflicts with a source you trust.
- **Data-sensitivity flags.** The task involves regulated personal data, confidential records, or anything whose mishandling has legal consequences.

If none of these apply — internal, low-impact, easily reversible, no sensitive specifics — a lighter self-review is proportionate.

## Worked examples

- **Marketing subject-line variations for A/B testing.** Low stakes, reversible, internal. A quick read is enough; no formal human review needed.
- **Summary of an employee's performance used in a termination decision.** Employment-affecting and consequential. Requires review by a qualified person (HR/legal) and verification of every factual claim against the record before any action.
- **A patient-facing explanation of a medication schedule.** Medical and safety-critical. A licensed professional must review before it reaches the patient; a fluent draft is not sufficient.
- **A cited compliance memo for a regulator.** Public-welfare and compliance context with fabrication-prone specifics. Verify each citation against the primary source and have a qualified reviewer sign off.

## Human review is a control, not a courtesy

The exam distinguishes real oversight from theater. Meaningful review means a competent person actually examines the substance — checks the facts, the reasoning, and the fit — and has the authority and time to change or reject the output. A rubber-stamp ("looked fine, approved") is not the control the policy intends. Similarly, "additional verification" means an *independent* check against authoritative sources, not asking Claude whether it is sure. The model's self-reported confidence is not a reliability signal and never a substitute for human judgment on a high-stakes item.

## A related control: handle sensitive data before use, not after

Escalation is not only about reviewing outputs; it is also about not creating avoidable risk on the way in. Before uploading regulated or confidential personal data, redact or anonymize it. Instructing Claude "don't retain this" is not a substitute for a real data control — the safeguard is what you do to the data, governed by your organization's policy, not a request to the model. When a task would require exposing sensitive data you're not authorized to share, that itself is a trigger to escalate to the appropriate owner rather than proceed.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- **Treating a fluent, confident output as review-complete.** Polish and confidence are not verification; consequential items still need a qualified human.
- **Applying heavy review everywhere, or nowhere.** Both waste effort or invite harm — the point is to triage by stakes.
- **Letting Claude "verify itself."** Independent human or source-based checks are the requirement, not self-attestation.
- **Escalating the output but ignoring the input.** Sensitive data should be controlled before it ever reaches the tool.
- **Assuming B2B or internal use removes the duty.** Consequential, individual-affecting decisions warrant human oversight regardless of channel.

**The rule:** ask "if this is wrong, who is harmed and how badly, and can it be undone?" The higher and less reversible the stakes — and the more regulated the domain — the more a qualified human must review and independently verify before anything is finalized.
