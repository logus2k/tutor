# Apply Claude to Analyze Requirements and Use Cases

Before Claude drafts a single deliverable, the more valuable work is often earlier: turning a vague business goal into a clear, testable set of requirements, and honestly judging whether an AI assistant is even the right tool. This topic tests whether you can use Claude as a thinking partner for scoping — and whether you can tell a good AI fit from a bad one.

## Using Claude to clarify a fuzzy request

Most real requests arrive underspecified: "Help us improve onboarding," "We need a better weekly report," "Can AI handle our support inbox?" A strong practitioner does not immediately ask Claude to produce output. They first use Claude to *interrogate the request*.

A reliable move is to ask Claude to act as a business analyst and generate clarifying questions before proposing anything: "I want to redesign our customer onboarding email sequence. Before you draft anything, ask me the 8–10 questions you'd need answered to scope this well." Claude will surface the audience, the success metric, constraints (brand voice, compliance, length), the current baseline, and edge cases. This converts a one-line ask into a structured brief.

The wrong-but-tempting alternative is to let Claude *assume* the missing details and start generating. It will happily invent a plausible audience and tone, and you will spend more time correcting a confident wrong draft than you saved. On the exam, "ask Claude to draft it right away" is usually the trap answer; "have Claude elicit and confirm requirements first" is the disciplined one.

## Eliciting and structuring requirements

Once you have raw context — meeting notes, a Slack thread, stakeholder emails — Claude is effective at *structuring* it into requirements you can act on and verify.

Practical patterns:

- **Extract and categorize.** Paste messy notes and ask Claude to separate functional requirements ("the report must show week-over-week revenue"), constraints ("must fit on one page"), assumptions, and open questions. Making assumptions and open questions explicit is what prevents silent scope drift.
- **Draft acceptance criteria.** Ask Claude to convert each requirement into a checkable statement: "Done when the summary is under 200 words, cites the source dashboard, and flags any metric that moved more than 10%." Testable criteria are what let you later judge whether the solution actually worked.
- **Prioritize.** Ask Claude to sort requirements into must-have / should-have / nice-to-have and to name the tradeoffs. Treat its ranking as a draft to react to, not a verdict.
- **Find gaps and contradictions.** "Read these requirements as a skeptical reviewer and list what's missing, ambiguous, or self-contradictory." Claude is good at spotting that two stakeholders asked for opposite things.

Use a **Project** when the requirements work spans several sessions: load the source documents and a custom instruction ("You are our requirements analyst; always separate assumptions from confirmed facts") so the context persists and teammates can build on the same shared understanding rather than re-explaining each time.

## Assessing whether a task is a good AI fit

The exam explicitly rewards the judgment of *when not to use AI, or where to keep a human in the loop*. A task is generally a **good fit** for Claude when it involves language, synthesis, drafting, transformation, or reasoning over text you can supply, AND a knowledgeable human can verify the output. Strong-fit examples: summarizing long documents, drafting first-pass content, restructuring notes, comparing options, generating test data, explaining a concept, converting a format.

A task is a **poor or high-risk fit** when it has one or more of these traits:

- **It needs a single, authoritative, checkable fact and no one will verify it.** Because models can fabricate specific-looking details — citations, statute subsection numbers, figures — an unverified factual lookup for a compliance or legal audience is a bad fit. The output *looks* trustworthy, which is exactly the danger. Self-reported confidence ("I'm certain the regulation says X") is not a reliability signal.
- **It depends on private, regulated, or personal data that hasn't been handled correctly.** If the use case requires uploading customer PII, health, or financial records, the data-sensitivity control comes *first*: redact or anonymize before uploading, and confirm the workspace's data terms. Telling Claude "don't remember this" is not a substitute for a real policy control.
- **The decision has legal, safety, financial, or ethical weight and would be executed automatically.** Claude can *inform* a hiring, lending, medical, or disciplinary decision, but the human makes and owns it.
- **It requires real-time, ground-truth system access Claude doesn't have.** If the answer lives in a live database or a system with no connector, Claude will guess unless you supply the data.

A useful decision rule: **fit = (language/synthesis task) × (a human can and will verify the result) × (data is handled per policy).** If any factor is near zero, redesign the use case — narrow it, add a verification step, anonymize the data, or route it to a human — rather than forcing it.

## A worked mini-example

Request: "Use AI to answer our incoming HR benefits questions automatically."

Applying the analysis: the *drafting* of answers from an approved benefits document is a good fit (language + synthesis). But *fully automatic* replies are a poor fit, because a wrong benefits answer has financial and trust consequences, and the benefits handbook contains details Claude could paraphrase incorrectly. The redesigned use case: Claude drafts a grounded answer from the uploaded handbook in a Project, cites the section, and an HR reviewer approves before it's sent. Same tool, but the human-in-the-loop and verification steps convert a risky idea into a sound one. This "narrow the scope and add verification" instinct is the core competency this topic tests.

## Common pitfalls the exam probes

- Treating Claude's first structured brief as final instead of confirming it with stakeholders.
- Skipping the "is this even a good AI task?" question and jumping to prompting.
- Uploading sensitive requirements documents without checking data handling.
- Accepting a fabricated-looking specific (a named regulation, a precise statistic) without verification because it sounds authoritative.
