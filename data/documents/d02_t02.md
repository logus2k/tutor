# Identify Hallucinations, Inconsistencies, and Biases in Responses

This topic sits at the center of the exam's heaviest domain because it tests a specific professional skill: recognizing *how* an AI output can be wrong even when it looks right. Knowing the failure modes — fabrication, contradiction, and bias — is what lets you catch a problem before it reaches a customer, a regulator, or a decision-maker.

## What a hallucination is

A hallucination is text that is factually incorrect or unsupported by the provided context, produced confidently as if it were true. Language models generate fluent, plausible language; fluency is not the same as accuracy. Anthropic states plainly that even advanced models like Claude can sometimes generate content that is factually wrong or inconsistent with the given context, and that techniques reduce but do not eliminate this.

The dangerous hallucinations are the *specific* ones. A vague generality is easy to distrust; a precise-looking detail invites belief. Watch especially for fabricated:

- **Citations and sources** — a paper, author, case, or URL that does not exist, or a real source cited for a claim it never makes.
- **Subsection and clause numbers** — "per Section 4.3.2 of the policy" when there is no 4.3.2, or the number points to unrelated text.
- **Quotes** — words placed in quotation marks that the source never contains.
- **Statistics and figures** — a confident "37% increase" with no basis in the provided data.
- **Names, dates, and identifiers** — plausible but invented people, product versions, or order numbers.

A worked example: you ask Claude to support a compliance memo and it writes, "This aligns with GDPR Article 17(2), which mandates 90-day deletion." Article 17 exists; the "90-day" specificity may be invented. The fabrication is hidden precisely because the surrounding claim is real. The evaluation move is to open the actual article and check the wording, not to be reassured that the article number is genuine.

## Internal inconsistencies

Some errors surface without any external reference, because the response contradicts itself. Line items that do not sum to the stated total; an executive summary that says "revenue grew" while the body says it declined; a recommendation in the conclusion that reverses the analysis above it. Reading a full response for internal consistency is a cheap, powerful check — a contradiction is proof that at least one part is unreliable, and it tells you exactly where to dig. Anthropic's guidance also suggests running the same prompt more than once (best-of-N) and comparing: where independent outputs disagree on a fact, that fact is a likely hallucination.

## Bias in responses

Bias is a distinct failure from fabrication: the facts may be defensible, but the framing, selection, or emphasis skews the output in a way that could mislead or unfairly affect people. Types to watch for:

- **Selection/coverage bias** — one option, vendor, or viewpoint is presented favorably while alternatives get thin treatment.
- **Stereotyping** — assumptions tied to gender, ethnicity, age, or other protected characteristics, especially in content about hiring, lending, housing, or performance.
- **Framing/anchoring bias** — the way you phrased the prompt ("explain why Plan A is best") steers Claude toward confirming your premise rather than testing it.
- **Cultural or demographic skew** — examples, names, or norms that assume one audience and exclude others.

Bias matters most in consequential, individual-affecting contexts. Anthropic's usage policy treats legal, financial, employment, and other high-risk uses as requiring extra care, precisely because biased or skewed output there can cause real harm.

## Self-reported confidence is not a reliability signal

This is one of the exam's clearest lessons. If Claude says "I'm confident," "definitely," or "I'm certain," that expression carries no evidentiary weight — a model can be equally fluent whether it is right or wrong, and it can be confidently wrong. Do not use the tone of certainty, or Claude's stated confidence level, as a proxy for correctness. Verification against an authoritative source is the only signal that counts. The tempting-but-wrong exam answer is "Claude said it was sure, so it's reliable"; the correct answer is "confidence is not verification — check it."

## How to surface these problems

- **Trace every specific to a source.** Citations, numbers, and quotes should point to something real; if you can't confirm it, treat it as unverified.
- **Ask Claude to support each claim with a quote, and retract claims it can't support.** This makes fabrication visible.
- **Give permission to say "I don't know."** Uncertainty admitted is far safer than a gap filled with invention.
- **Read for internal contradiction.** Reconcile totals, summaries, and conclusions against the details.
- **Re-run and compare.** Divergent answers to the same question flag instability.
- **Interrogate framing.** Ask "what's the strongest case against this?" to counter framing bias you may have introduced.

## Pitfalls the exam tests

- Believing a detail because it is *specific* — specificity is a red flag for fabrication, not a guarantee of truth.
- Trusting a citation because the source is real, without checking that it says what Claude claims.
- Treating a confident tone as accuracy.
- Missing bias because the individual facts are correct, when the *balance* is what misleads.

**The rule:** hallucinations are confident, specific, and unsupported; inconsistencies reveal themselves inside the text; bias hides in framing and emphasis. None are cured by the model sounding sure. Verify specifics against authoritative sources — especially for compliance and high-stakes audiences.
