# Create Effective Prompts for Business and Technical Tasks

A prompt is the instruction you give Claude, and the quality of that instruction is the single biggest lever you control over the quality of the result. The Foundations exam treats prompting as a professional skill: it rewards candidates who can translate a real business or technical goal into a clear, well-scoped request, and it penalizes the habit of typing a vague one-liner and hoping for the best.

## The core mental model

Anthropic's own guidance frames it simply: treat Claude like a brilliant but brand-new colleague who is capable but has no context on your norms, your audience, or your definition of "good." A new hire who is handed "make me a dashboard" will guess; a new hire who is told what the dashboard is for, who will read it, and what it must contain will deliver something usable. The same is true of Claude. The more precisely you explain what you want, the closer the first draft lands.

A useful test comes straight from the docs: show your prompt to a person who lacks context and ask them to follow it. If they would be confused, Claude will be too. That "confused colleague" check is exactly the kind of judgment the exam is probing.

## The building blocks of a strong prompt

Effective prompts combine several ingredients. You rarely need all of them, but knowing each one lets you choose deliberately.

**Clarity and explicit instructions.** Be specific and direct about what you want. "Summarize this contract" is weaker than "Summarize this contract in five bullet points, flagging any clause about auto-renewal or early termination." If you want above-and-beyond effort, ask for it explicitly rather than assuming Claude will infer ambition from a terse prompt.

**Context and motivation.** Explaining *why* you want something helps Claude generalize to your actual goal. The docs give a memorable example: instead of "never use ellipses," say "this will be read aloud by a text-to-speech engine, so never use ellipses because it cannot pronounce them." Once Claude knows the reason, it makes better decisions about edge cases you did not spell out. In business terms, tell Claude the audience (a skeptical CFO, a new customer, a regulator) and the purpose (persuade, inform, decide).

**Role or persona.** Assigning Claude a role focuses its tone and expertise. "You are a compliance analyst reviewing vendor agreements" primes different behavior than "you are a friendly marketing copywriter." A single sentence of role-setting measurably shifts the register and the priorities of the output.

**Output format.** State the shape you want: a table, a three-paragraph memo, a numbered checklist, an email ready to send. A reliable technique from the docs is to tell Claude what to do rather than what not to do — "write in flowing prose paragraphs" works better than "don't use bullet points." When order or completeness matters, ask for sequential numbered steps.

**Examples (few-shot / multishot).** Showing one to a few examples of the input-and-desired-output pattern is one of the most reliable ways to steer format, tone, and structure. Anthropic recommends three to five examples that are *relevant* (mirror your real case), *diverse* (cover edge cases so Claude does not latch onto an accidental pattern), and clearly separated from your instructions. If you have a "good" past deliverable, pasting it as a model is often faster than describing the style in words.

**Constraints.** Length limits, tone rules, things to include or exclude, and boundaries ("only use the figures in the document I uploaded; do not add outside numbers") keep the output inside the lines. Constraints are also a safety tool: they are where you tell Claude not to invent citations or not to speculate beyond the source.

## Business framing versus technical framing

The exam candidate is a business professional, and prompts for business tasks tend to emphasize *audience, purpose, and tone*. A prompt to draft a stakeholder update should name who the stakeholders are, what decision the update supports, how formal it should read, and what length fits the channel. The success criteria are persuasion, clarity, and appropriateness, so the prompt should encode those.

Technical tasks — analyzing a spreadsheet, structuring data, outlining a process, drafting documentation — tend to emphasize *precision, correctness, and structure*. Here you lean harder on explicit steps, exact output formats, and instructions to ground the answer in provided material. At the Foundations altitude this still means natural-language prompting in the product, not code: you are asking Claude to reason over documents or data you supply, and you are specifying the structure of the answer, not writing an API call.

Both framings share the same skeleton — clarity, context, format, constraints — but weight the ingredients differently.

## A worked mini-example

Weak: "Write a product launch announcement."

Strong: "You are our product marketing lead. Write a launch announcement email for our existing small-business customers announcing a new invoicing feature. Goal: drive them to try it this week. Tone: warm, confident, jargon-free. Length: under 150 words with one clear call-to-action button text. Include one sentence on the specific time it saves. Do not overstate capabilities or invent statistics."

The strong version names the role, audience, goal, tone, length, required content, and a guardrail against fabrication. That is the difference the exam is testing.

## Common pitfalls the exam rewards you for avoiding

- **The vague one-liner.** Assuming Claude will infer your audience and standards. The fix is context and explicit instructions.
- **Piling everything into one giant ask.** For genuinely multi-part work, decomposing into steps (a separate topic) produces better results than one overloaded prompt.
- **Trusting confident-sounding specifics.** Claude can produce fabricated citations, figures, or clause numbers that look authoritative. Self-reported confidence is not a reliability signal. For anything high-stakes or compliance-related, a good prompt asks Claude to ground claims in the provided source, and you still verify factual details before you send.
- **Skipping data-sensitivity checks.** Redact or anonymize regulated personal data *before* uploading it. Telling Claude "don't keep this" is not a substitute for applying the actual privacy control.
- **Wrong-but-tempting model choice.** Reaching for the most powerful tier for every task wastes cost and speed; match the tier to the difficulty. Simple, high-volume drafting does not need the top model.

Mastering these building blocks — and knowing which to emphasize for a business versus a technical goal — is the foundation everything else in this domain builds on.
