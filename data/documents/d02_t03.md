# Apply Fact-Checking and Validation Techniques

Spotting that an output *might* be wrong is only half the skill; the exam's heaviest domain also tests whether you know the concrete techniques to *confirm* whether it is right. This topic is the practical toolkit: verifying against authoritative sources, cross-checking, using citations and connectors, and spot-checking quantitative results.

## Verify against authoritative sources

The default validation move is to compare a claim against a trustworthy, independent source of truth — the original contract, the official statistic, the primary document, the system of record. "Authoritative" means the source that would settle the question in a dispute, not a convenient echo of the claim. Two principles matter:

- **Prefer primary over secondary.** Check the actual policy text, not a paraphrase of it. Anthropic's own hallucination guidance recommends having Claude work from provided documents and grounding claims in the real wording rather than general recall.
- **Match the source to the stakes.** A low-stakes internal note may need only a quick sanity check; a customer-facing or regulated claim needs the definitive source.

## Cross-checking

Cross-checking validates a claim by confirming it from more than one independent angle. Techniques include:

- **Multiple sources.** Confirm a figure in two independent places; agreement raises confidence, disagreement flags a problem.
- **Best-of-N / regeneration.** Anthropic suggests running the same prompt several times and comparing. If independent generations disagree on a fact, that fact is unstable and likely fabricated. Consistency is necessary but not sufficient — a model can be consistently wrong — so pair it with an external check.
- **Reverse verification.** After Claude answers, ask it to find a supporting quote for each claim and to retract any claim it cannot support. This converts an open-ended answer into an auditable one.
- **Independent recomputation.** Redo a calculation yourself, or in a separate tool, rather than trusting the narrated arithmetic.

## Use citations and grounding

Citations make an output checkable. When Claude cites the exact passage behind each claim, validation collapses from "is this true in general?" to "does this passage say what Claude says it does?" — a fast, concrete check. Anthropic offers grounding both as a prompting practice (ask for quotes and sources for every claim) and, on its platform, as a Citations capability that ties answers to exact passages in source documents so the response is auditable.

Two cautions the exam rewards:

1. **A citation is a pointer, not proof.** Prompt-based citations can themselves be fabricated. Open the source and confirm it exists and actually supports the claim. Never treat the presence of a footnote as verification.
2. **Grounding narrows but doesn't remove risk.** Even grounded answers can misread the passage; spot-check the mapping between claim and quote.

## Use connectors and research mode — then verify the trail

Claude's connectors (Google Drive, Gmail, Google Calendar, and others) and its agentic Research mode let Claude pull from your actual documents, email, and the live web, and return answers with citations that link back to the specific emails, files, or pages used. This is a strong validation aid: instead of relying on the model's memory, you can follow each citation to the original and confirm it. Research mode can run an extended, multi-step investigation and assemble a cited report.

The judgment point: connectors improve *traceability*, not *infallibility*. Claude might cite a real document but summarize it slightly wrong, or lean on an unreliable web page. Validation still means opening the cited source and confirming it says what the answer claims — and, for the open web, judging whether the source itself is trustworthy.

## Spot-check quantitative output

Numbers deserve special discipline because they look objective and are easy to fabricate.

- **Recompute a sample.** Re-add a column, re-derive a percentage, check that parts sum to the whole.
- **Sanity-check magnitude.** Does the number pass a "could this even be true?" test against what you know? A "220% market share" is impossible on its face.
- **Check units and time frames.** Confusions between thousands and millions, or monthly and annual, are common and consequential.
- **Trace figures to the data.** Every number in a summary should map to a value in the underlying source. If it doesn't appear there, treat it as invented until proven otherwise.

A worked example: Claude produces a budget table whose category lines total $480k but whose stated grand total is $500k. The internal mismatch is immediate evidence of an error; you recompute, find the true total, and do not forward the table until it reconciles.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- **Treating a citation or a confident tone as verification.** Neither is. Open the source; confidence is not a reliability signal.
- **Validating a claim with the model that made it, and stopping there.** Asking Claude "are you sure?" is not independent verification; the check must be external or reproducible.
- **Confusing consistency with truth.** Repeated agreement across regenerations can still be uniformly wrong; anchor at least one check to an authoritative source.
- **Skipping the arithmetic because the table looks tidy.** Formatting is not correctness — spot-check the numbers.
- **Verifying prose but trusting the footnotes.** Fabricated references are among the most common and most damaging hallucinations.

**The rule:** validate proportionally to the stakes, ground claims in primary sources, and confirm — don't assume — that citations and numbers hold up. Connectors and citations make verification *easier*; they never make it *optional*.
