# Edit, Adapt, Refine, and Compare Outputs for the Intended Audience

A verified, accurate output is still not finished if it doesn't fit the person who will read it. This topic tests a working professional's craft: taking a correct first draft and shaping it — tone, level, length, format — for a specific audience, then iterating and comparing variants until it's right. On the exam it pairs naturally with evaluation: you verify that an output is *true*, then refine it so it is *usable*.

## Start from the audience, not the draft

Before editing a word, define who will read this and what they need. The same underlying facts should be expressed very differently for different readers:

- **Executive / decision-maker:** lead with the recommendation and the "so what," keep it short, minimize jargon, surface risks and trade-offs.
- **Technical specialist / peer:** precision, detail, and correct terminology matter more than brevity.
- **Customer / external audience:** plain language, warm and on-brand tone, no internal shorthand, nothing unverified.
- **General or non-expert reader:** define terms, use analogies, shorter sentences.

The dimensions you adapt are consistent: **tone** (formal, friendly, authoritative), **reading level and jargon**, **length and depth**, **structure and format** (email, memo, bullet brief, table, slide outline), and **emphasis** (what leads, what's cut). The most common miss is a technically correct output pitched at the wrong altitude — accurate, but unusable for the reader.

## Direct Claude to adapt, don't rewrite by hand

The efficient path is to tell Claude the audience and constraints and let it re-render. Effective refinement prompts name the reader and the change: "Rewrite this for a non-technical executive: three sentences, lead with the recommendation, no acronyms," or "Adapt this policy summary for frontline staff at an 8th-grade reading level." Giving Claude a role or persona ("as a customer-support lead writing to a frustrated client") and a concrete constraint (word count, format, tone) produces far better adaptation than a vague "make it better."

Refinement is **iterative**. Anthropic's guidance frames improving outputs as a loop: use the output as the input to a follow-up prompt, review, and adjust. Practical iteration moves include "make it half as long," "add a short example for each point," "soften the tone," "turn the prose into a comparison table," and "flag anything you're unsure about so I can verify." Each pass converges the draft on the target.

## Compare variants deliberately

For anything important, generate more than one version and compare, rather than accepting the first. Two ways to do this:

- **Ask for several variants at once** — e.g., three subject lines, or a formal and a conversational version of the same announcement — and choose or blend.
- **Regenerate and contrast** — produce the answer a second time and compare framing, coverage, and tone.

Comparison serves two goals. First, *quality selection*: pick the version that best fits the audience. Second, *validation*: as covered elsewhere in this domain, when independent versions disagree on a fact, that disagreement flags a possible hallucination to verify. So comparing variants sharpens both fit and accuracy.

Compare against explicit criteria, not gut feel: Which version fully covers the ask? Which matches the required tone and length? Which is easiest for *this* reader? Deciding the criteria before you compare keeps the choice objective.

## Worked mini-example

You have an accurate internal analysis of why a project slipped. For the **project team**, you refine it into a detailed retrospective with root causes and action items. For the **VP**, you ask Claude to compress the same content into a five-line update: status, impact, cause, fix, date. For the **client**, you adapt it again into a brief, reassuring note that omits internal blame and commits to next steps. One verified set of facts, three audience-shaped outputs. Editing does not change the truth; it changes the delivery — and you re-verify that no adaptation introduced a new unsupported claim.

## Refinement must preserve accuracy

Editing for audience can quietly break correctness: shortening may drop a necessary caveat; simplifying may overstate a nuanced finding; a punchier tone may turn a hedge into a promise. After each refinement, re-check that the adapted version is still faithful to the verified facts and still complete for the ask. Adaptation is a rewrite of *form*, and every rewrite is a chance for a new error to enter.

Tools help here. **Artifacts** are well suited to content you'll iterate on — a document or brief in a side panel you can revise across turns while the chat stays a workspace. **Projects** let you store audience context, brand voice, and style guidelines as custom instructions and knowledge, so Claude adapts consistently to your standing audiences without re-explaining each time.

## Common pitfalls the exam tests

- **Shipping the first draft.** The first version is a starting point; refining and comparing beats accepting it as-is.
- **Editing for style but forgetting the audience's actual need.** Prettier prose aimed at the wrong reader is still a miss.
- **Letting adaptation corrupt accuracy.** Re-verify after refining; simplification can introduce overstatement.
- **Vague refinement prompts.** "Improve this" underperforms a named audience plus a concrete constraint.
- **Comparing on vibes.** Judge variants against explicit criteria (coverage, tone, length, fit), not a hunch.

**The rule:** define the audience first, direct Claude to adapt tone/level/length/format to them, iterate in passes, and compare variants against explicit criteria — all while re-verifying that each refinement stays faithful to the facts.
