# Integrate Claude into Existing Workflows to Augment or Redesign Them

The goal of adopting Claude is rarely "use AI"; it's to make an existing workflow faster, more consistent, or higher quality. This topic tests whether you can decide *where* Claude belongs in a real process, whether to **augment** the current flow or **redesign** it, and how to keep the right human checkpoints so the result is trustworthy.

## Augment versus redesign

There are two fundamentally different integration strategies, and choosing correctly is a core judgment the exam probes.

**Augment** means keeping the workflow's shape and inserting Claude into specific steps to make them faster or better. The process, roles, and hand-offs stay the same; Claude drafts, summarizes, triages, or checks within them. This is lower-risk, quick to adopt, and reversible. Example: a support team keeps its existing ticket queue and reviewer, but Claude now drafts the first-pass reply for the agent to edit and send.

**Redesign** means rethinking the workflow because AI makes a fundamentally different shape possible. Steps get removed, merged, or reordered; the human role shifts from *doing* to *reviewing*. This unlocks bigger gains but carries more change-management and risk. Example: instead of a monthly report assembled by hand, a team builds a repeatable Project where Claude drafts the whole narrative from supplied data, and the analyst's job becomes verifying and approving rather than writing.

The decision rule: **start by augmenting; redesign only when the augmented version reveals that the old process shape is the real bottleneck.** The tempting wrong answer is to redesign everything on day one — "let AI run the whole process." That maximizes risk, resistance, and the blast radius of any error before you've learned where Claude is actually reliable. Prove value in the small, augmented version first.

## Where in a workflow to insert Claude

Not every step is a good insertion point. Map the workflow as discrete steps and evaluate each one. Claude fits best where a step is:

- **Language- or synthesis-heavy** — drafting, summarizing, rewriting, extracting, classifying, comparing.
- **Repeatable and well-defined** — the same kind of task recurs with clear inputs and a checkable output. These are also the best automation candidates.
- **A bottleneck** — the step where work piles up or rework happens.
- **Verifiable** — a knowledgeable human can confirm the output, or the cost of an error is low.

Claude fits poorly where a step **requires live system access it doesn't have** (it will guess unless you supply the data or a connector), where it **turns on private or regulated data that hasn't been anonymized or cleared for use**, or where the step is a **high-stakes final decision** that must be owned by a person. **Connectors** (Google Drive, Gmail, Calendar, and others) can bridge some data-access gaps by letting Claude reference approved external knowledge with permission — but connecting a system is a decision with data-governance weight, not a convenience toggle.

A practical placement heuristic: put Claude on the *drafting and triage* edges of a workflow and keep humans on the *decision and release* edges.

## Human-in-the-loop: the non-negotiable

The exam consistently rewards keeping a human checkpoint where it matters. "Human-in-the-loop" means a person reviews or approves before an AI-influenced output has real-world effect. Where to place the checkpoint depends on stakes:

- **High stakes** (legal, financial, medical, HR, anything customer-facing and irreversible, anything for a compliance audience): mandatory human review before release. Because Claude can produce specific-looking but fabricated details, and its confident tone is not a reliability signal, the reviewer verifies the load-bearing facts against an authoritative source.
- **Medium stakes** (internal drafts, routine external replies): lighter review, perhaps spot-checks.
- **Low stakes, high volume** (tagging, routing, first-draft summaries seen only internally): a person can supervise in aggregate rather than checking each item.

Design the checkpoint into the workflow explicitly — "agent edits and approves every reply before it sends" — rather than hoping someone catches errors. A workflow that lets unreviewed AI output reach customers or regulators is a design flaw, not a speed feature.

## Identifying automation opportunities

To find where Claude helps most, look across your regular work for tasks that are: repetitive, language-based, well-defined, time-consuming, and tolerant of a verification step. Ask, for each candidate: *What is the input? What is the output? Who verifies it? What breaks if it's wrong?* If the input is supplyable text, the output is checkable, and the failure cost is manageable, it's a strong candidate.

Make the improvement durable by capturing it as a reusable asset — a Project with fixed instructions and knowledge, an Artifact template, or a saved workflow — so the gain repeats instead of depending on one person re-prompting from scratch each time. Match the model tier to the step: Haiku for high-volume simple triage, Sonnet as the default workhorse, Opus for the genuinely complex reasoning steps; using the top tier for routine steps wastes cost and latency.

### A worked mini-example

Current workflow: incoming sales leads are manually read, categorized, and routed, then a rep writes a tailored intro email.

- **Augment first:** Claude classifies and routes each lead (a simple, high-volume step — Haiku-tier) and drafts the intro email from a template. The rep reviews and sends. The process shape is unchanged; two slow steps got faster, and the human still owns the send.
- **Then consider redesign:** if triage proves reliable over weeks, connect the CRM so Claude drafts directly from lead context, and shift the rep from "writer" to "approver" — a genuine redesign, adopted only after the augmented version earned trust.

The insertion points were the *repeatable, verifiable, language-heavy* steps; the human stayed on the *decision to send*; and redesign came *after* evidence, not before. That sequencing — augment, verify, keep humans on decisions, redesign only when justified — is the judgment this topic tests.

## Common pitfalls the exam probes

- Jumping straight to full redesign/automation before proving value with augmentation.
- Inserting Claude into a step that needs live data or a final human decision.
- Removing the human checkpoint on high-stakes or compliance-facing output.
- Connecting sensitive systems or uploading regulated data without governance.
- Leaving the improvement as a one-off prompt instead of a reusable Project or template.
