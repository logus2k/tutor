# Iterate Prompts to Improve Output Quality

Very few prompts land perfectly on the first try, and the exam does not expect them to. What it expects is that you treat prompting as a loop: read the output critically, diagnose what fell short, adjust the prompt, and try again. The skill being tested is not writing one magic prompt but knowing how to close the gap between what you got and what you needed.

## Iteration is the normal workflow, not a failure

Anthropic frames prompt engineering as inherently empirical: you start with a first draft, test it against your success criteria, and refine. The corollary is that a disappointing first answer is data, not a dead end. The professional move is to ask "what specifically is wrong, and which lever fixes it?" rather than either accepting a weak result or scrapping Claude entirely.

This also means you should have a rough idea of "good" before you start. If you cannot say what a successful output looks like — the audience, the format, the must-haves — you cannot tell whether iteration is helping. Defining success criteria first is what makes the loop measurable.

## Diagnosing a weak output

Before changing anything, pinpoint the failure. Different weaknesses call for different fixes, and guessing wastes turns. Common diagnoses and their remedies:

- **Wrong format or structure** (a wall of text when you wanted a table; bullets when you wanted prose). Fix: state the format explicitly, and tell Claude what to do rather than what to avoid — "write in flowing paragraphs" beats "don't use lists." Better still, show an example of the layout you want.
- **Right shape, wrong substance — too shallow or generic.** Fix: add context about audience and purpose, and explicitly request depth ("go beyond the basics; cover edge cases"). Vague prompts yield vague answers.
- **Missed a requirement.** Fix: the requirement was probably buried. Break the instructions into an explicit numbered list so nothing hides inside a run-on sentence.
- **Wrong tone or register.** Fix: assign a role ("you are a compliance officer") and name the tone and reader directly.
- **Inconsistent results across runs.** Fix: add examples. Few-shot examples are one of the most reliable ways to pin down format, tone, and structure so outputs stop drifting.
- **Fabricated specifics** — invented citations, figures, or clause numbers that look authoritative. Fix: instruct Claude to ground claims in the provided source and to say when it does not know, and then verify the specifics yourself. Self-reported confidence is never proof of accuracy.

The discipline here mirrors a broader rule: diagnose by looking at the actual output, not by assuming why it went wrong. Read what Claude actually produced and match the fix to the real defect.

## The levers you adjust

When you refine, you are usually pulling one of a small set of levers:

**Sharpen the instructions.** Make the ask more specific and explicit. Replace "improve this" with "make this two sentences shorter and remove the passive voice." Add the *why* so Claude can generalize — telling it the output will be read aloud, or sent to a wary client, shapes dozens of small decisions you would otherwise have to spell out.

**Add or improve examples.** If describing what you want is not landing, show it. Paste a past deliverable that hit the mark, or a small input-output pair, and Claude will mirror the pattern. Three to five relevant, varied examples work better than one, and better than a paragraph of adjectives.

**Add constraints.** Length caps, inclusion and exclusion rules, "use only the numbers in the document," "no marketing superlatives." Constraints tighten a loose output and double as guardrails against fabrication and scope creep.

**Restructure the request.** If one prompt keeps producing a muddled result, the problem may be that it is doing too much. Break it into ordered steps or separate turns so each part gets focused handling.

## Refine in the prompt or in a follow-up turn?

You have two ways to iterate, and choosing well matters.

**Follow-up turns** are fast and conversational: "Good, but make it half the length and add a line about pricing." Because Claude keeps the conversation in view, you can steer incrementally without rewriting everything. This is ideal for polishing a specific output and for exploratory back-and-forth where you are discovering what you want as you go.

**Editing the original prompt** is the better move when you have learned something reusable — a format requirement or a constraint you will want *every* time, or when the conversation has accumulated so many patches that it has become messy and self-contradictory. Folding the lessons back into a clean, improved prompt gives you a repeatable starting point rather than a fragile thread you cannot reproduce. A common pattern: iterate via follow-ups to discover the right instructions, then consolidate those instructions into one solid prompt (or a saved Project instruction) for reuse.

The tempting-but-wrong habit is to keep bolting correction after correction onto an increasingly tangled conversation. Past a point, the accumulated contradictions confuse rather than clarify; starting fresh with a cleaned-up prompt is faster and more reliable.

## The iterate loop in practice

A compact worked example. First attempt: "Write a summary of this policy document." The result is a generic five-paragraph essay. Diagnosis: too long, wrong audience, no structure. Refinement: "Summarize this policy for frontline managers who have two minutes. Five bullets, each one action they must take. Plain language, no legal jargon. Only include rules stated in the document." The result is closer but invents a deadline that is not in the source. Diagnosis: fabrication. Final refinement: add "If the document does not state a date, write 'no date specified' — do not infer one," and then verify the bullets against the text before distributing. Three passes, each fixing a specific, observed defect — that is the loop.

## Common pitfalls the exam probes

- **Changing many things at once**, so you cannot tell which edit helped. Adjust deliberately.
- **Treating a fluent, confident answer as a correct one.** Verify factual specifics, especially for compliance or high-stakes audiences, regardless of how assured the wording sounds.
- **Endlessly patching a broken thread** instead of consolidating into a fresh, clean prompt.
- **Iterating without a target.** If you have not defined what "good" means, you are polishing blind.

Iteration, done as a deliberate diagnose-adjust-retest loop, is how ordinary prompts become reliable ones — and recognizing the right fix for a given failure is precisely the judgment the exam is measuring.
